Active galactic nuclei

An active galactic nucleus (AGN) is the centre of a galaxy that outshines the rest of it. At its heart is a supermassive black hole, millions to billions of solar masses, swallowing gas through an accretion disk and turning a large fraction of the rest-mass energy into light. The brightest AGN, the quasars, reach about $10^{14}\,L_\odot\approx4\times10^{47}$ erg/s, around 5000 times the luminosity of the whole Milky Way, from a region smaller than a light-year.

The central engine

Everything in the spectrum above is powered by gas falling into a black hole. This chapter covers the black hole itself, how its mass is measured, how much power accretion can deliver, and how gas actually spirals in.

Black holes in brief

The idea is older than relativity. In 1783 Michell reasoned that if light is made of particles obeying Newtonian gravity, a star whose escape velocity $v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}$ exceeds $c$ would be invisible, a "dark star", which happens at $R=2GM/c^2$. That this matches the Schwarzschild radius is a coincidence of the Newtonian argument: in general relativity light is not slowed down and turned back like a projectile, the horizon is a causal boundary.

\begin{aligned}ds^2&=-\left(1-\frac{r_S}{r}\right)c^2dt^2\\ &\quad+\left(1-\frac{r_S}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2+r^2d\Omega^2,\\ r_S&=\frac{2GM}{c^2}\approx3\,\mathrm{km}\left(\frac{M}{M_\odot}\right)\end{aligned}

It is convenient to measure distances in the gravitational radius $r_g=GM/c^2=r_S/2\approx1.5\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{cm}\,(M/10^8M_\odot)$, about one astronomical unit for a $10^8M_\odot$ hole. A clock deep in the potential runs slow, $dt=(1-r_S/r)^{-1/2}d\tau$, and light climbing out is redshifted by the same factor, $1+z=(1-r_S/r)^{-1/2}$, which diverges at the horizon. This gravitational redshift gives the red wing of the relativistic iron line (Sect. X-ray emission).

Gravitational redshift and time dilation near a black hole

Light emitted at radius $r$ by a static source arrives at infinity with $1+z=(1-r_S/r)^{-1/2}$, and the source's clock runs slow by the same factor. The effect is small beyond a few tens of $r_S$ but grows quickly in the last few gravitational radii, and diverges at the horizon: this is the origin of the red wing of the iron line.

The effective potential of orbits around the hole gives the characteristic radii. Massive particles have circular orbits only beyond the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), $6\,r_g$ for a non-rotating hole, inside which gas plunges. The marginally bound orbit ($4\,r_g$) is the circular orbit with total energy $mc^2$, and the photon sphere ($3\,r_g$) holds unstable circular photon orbits, which sets the black hole shadow of apparent radius $\sqrt{27}\,r_g\approx5.2\,r_g$, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope. The binding energy at the ISCO, $1-\sqrt{8/9}\approx5.7\%$ of the rest mass, is the radiative efficiency of a thin disk around a non-rotating hole.

Why there is an innermost stable orbit

The effective potential per unit mass, $V_{\rm eff}=-GM/r+\ell^2/(2r^2)-GM\ell^2/(c^2r^3)$, for different specific angular momenta $\ell$. The last term, which exists only in general relativity, makes the potential plunge at small $r$. Circular orbits sit at the extrema: stable at a minimum, unstable at a maximum. For $\ell<2\sqrt3\,GM/c$ there is no extremum at all and gas falls in; at $\ell=2\sqrt3\,GM/c$ the two merge at the ISCO, $6\,r_g$. The curve with $\ell=4\,GM/c$ has its maximum at exactly zero energy ($E=mc^2$): the marginally bound orbit at $4\,r_g$.

A real black hole is described by its mass and angular momentum alone (Kerr 1963; charge is negligible astrophysically). The dimensionless spin $a=cJ/(GM^2)$ lies between $-1$ and $1$. Rotation drags space around with it (frame dragging, the gravitational analogue of the magnetic field of a moving charge) and creates an ergosphere, between the horizon and the static limit, where nothing can stand still. Rotational energy can be extracted there by the Penrose process, or through magnetic fields by the Blandford–Znajek mechanism (Sect. Jets). Prograde orbits can reach much deeper than retrograde ones, so gas around a fast-spinning hole releases far more energy.

The price of spin: ISCO radius and radiative efficiency

The closer gas can orbit, the deeper in the potential well it radiates from, and the more of its rest-mass energy it releases. Going from a retrograde disk around a maximally spinning hole to a prograde one raises the efficiency by a factor of eleven, from 3.8% to 42%. This is why the radiative efficiency $\eta$ is a function of spin, and why the canonical $\eta=0.1$ is only a round number.

Weighing black holes

Black holes come in two very different families. Stellar-mass black holes are remnants of massive stars: the core burns H, He, C, Ne, O and Si in successive shells around an inert iron core (iron-group nuclei have the highest binding energy per nucleon, so fusion stops releasing energy), then collapses. What it leaves behind depends on the mass, as the figure below shows. Cores above the Chandrasekhar limit ($\approx1.4M_\odot$) collapse in a supernova, and above the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (about $2.2$ to $2.5\,M_\odot$ for realistic equations of state; the heaviest measured neutron star is $\approx2.1M_\odot$) nothing halts the collapse. Counting gives some $5\times10^7$ to $10^8$ stellar-mass black holes in the Milky Way. Supermassive black holes, from $10^6$ to $10^{10}M_\odot$, sit in galaxy centres and power AGN.

Several methods weigh black holes, and which one works depends on the mass and the distance (figure below). In X-ray binaries the Doppler semi-amplitude $K_*$ of the visible star gives a strict lower limit on the unseen companion's mass, independent of its nature, through the mass function:

\begin{aligned}f(M)&=\frac{P_{\rm orb}K_*^3}{2\pi G}\\ &=\frac{M_{\rm BH}^3\sin^3 i}{(M_{\rm BH}+M_*)^2}\le M_{\rm BH}\end{aligned}

The historical case is Cygnus X-1: found as an X-ray source in 1964, shown to vary on millisecond timescales (so $R\lesssim300$ km, not a normal star), and in 1972 shown to orbit the supergiant HDE 226868 ($P_{\rm orb}\approx5.6$ d) with a companion of at least $3M_\odot$ (today $\approx21M_\odot$, well above the TOV limit). Quiescent X-ray transients, whose companions dominate the optical light, give clean radial-velocity curves, so most dynamical masses come from them. Gravitational waves weigh merging black holes (GW150914: $36+29\to62M_\odot$, radiating $\approx3M_\odot c^2$), and Gaia finds dormant ones in wide binaries from astrometric orbits (BH1 $\approx9.6$, BH2 $\approx8.9$, BH3 $33M_\odot$).

For supermassive holes, Sgr A$^*$ is the best case: Keplerian orbits of the S-stars (S2 has $P\approx16$ yr and a pericentre of about 120 au) give $4.3\times10^6M_\odot$, work recognised by the 2020 Nobel Prize to Genzel and Ghez. In other galaxies, stellar, gas or maser kinematics work only if one resolves the black hole's sphere of influence, where its potential dominates over the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ of the stars:

\begin{aligned}r_{\rm infl}&\simeq\frac{GM_{\rm BH}}{\sigma^2}\\ &\approx4.3\,\mathrm{pc}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^7M_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma}{100\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}\right)^{-2}\end{aligned}

For $10^8M_\odot$ and $\sigma=200$ km/s at 10 Mpc this is $\approx11$ pc, or $0.2''$, which is why Hubble or adaptive optics limit the method to nearby galaxies. Beyond them, water masers (NGC 4258: $4\times10^7M_\odot$ and a geometric distance of 7.6 Mpc), interferometry (GRAVITY resolving the broad-line region of 3C 273) and the shadow images of the Event Horizon Telescope (M87, $6.5\times10^9M_\odot$; Sgr A$^*$) take over, and for distant quasars one relies on reverberation mapping and virial masses (Sect. Broad-line region).

Accretion power and the Eddington limit

What can power a quasar? 3C 273, the first one identified (Schmidt 1963, $z=0.158$), radiates $L\sim10^{46}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}\sim100\,L_{\rm MW}$ from a point source and varies on year timescales (Smith and Hoffleit 1963), which limits its size to about a light-year. Only accretion onto a supermassive black hole is efficient enough: hydrogen fusion releases 0.7% of the rest-mass energy, whereas gas falling to the ISCO releases $\eta=1-E_{\rm ISCO}/mc^2$, between $0.038$ and $0.42$ depending on the spin, with $\eta=0.1$ a canonical value.

\begin{aligned}L&=\eta\dot Mc^2,\\ \dot M&=\frac{L}{\eta c^2}\\ &\approx1.8\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}\left(\frac{L}{10^{46}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)\left(\frac{\eta}{0.1}\right)^{-1}\end{aligned}

There is a ceiling to this power. Radiation pushes on free electrons through Thomson scattering, electrostatic coupling carries the push to the protons, and gravity pulls on each electron–proton pair. Both forces scale as $r^{-2}$, so the balance holds at every radius, at the Eddington luminosity:

\begin{aligned}L_{\rm Edd}&=\frac{4\pi GM_{\rm BH}m_pc}{\sigma_T}\\ &\approx1.26\times10^{38}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{M_\odot}\right)\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}},\\ \lambda_{\rm Edd}&=\frac{L_{\rm bol}}{L_{\rm Edd}}\end{aligned}

Above it radiation pressure halts the inflow that powers the source (assuming spherical symmetry, ionized hydrogen and Thomson opacity; it can be exceeded where radiation escapes along the poles, or with photon trapping in slim disks). A steady accretion-powered source must therefore have $L\le L_{\rm Edd}\propto M_{\rm BH}$, a lower limit on the mass, and the corresponding accretion rate is the Eddington rate:

\begin{aligned}M_{\rm BH}&\ge M_{\rm Edd}=\frac{\sigma_TL}{4\pi Gm_pc}\\ &\approx8\times10^7M_\odot\left(\frac{L}{10^{46}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}}\right),\\ \dot M_{\rm Edd}&=\frac{L_{\rm Edd}}{\eta c^2}\\ &\approx2.2\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^8M_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{\eta}{0.1}\right)^{-1}\end{aligned}
The Eddington limit in the luminosity–mass plane

Lines of constant Eddington ratio $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$ are parallel, since $L_{\rm Edd}\propto M_{\rm BH}$. The mass is a lower limit because the observed $L$ fixes a minimum $L_{\rm Edd}$: a lighter hole would sit in the forbidden region, and any $\lambda_{\rm Edd}<1$ only raises the inferred mass (follow the dashed line for $L=10^{46}$ erg/s). Quasars have $\lambda_{\rm Edd}\sim0.01$ to $1$, low-luminosity AGN far less; Sgr A$^*$ shines at $\sim10^{-9}$ of its Eddington luminosity. Beaming (blazars) and super-Eddington accretion are the caveats.

How gas falls in: Bondi flow and thin disks

Spherical accretion is the simplest case: gas at rest far away falls onto the hole. It is captured within the accretion radius, where gravity beats the gas's thermal energy, and it needs no radiation to fall (the flow is adiabatic; spherical accretion is radiatively inefficient). Bondi (1952) assumed a steady, spherically symmetric polytropic gas $P=K\rho^\gamma$ with no angular momentum. Combining mass conservation and the Euler equation gives one equation for the Mach number $\mathcal M=v/c_s$ as a function of radius, with a special point where $\mathcal M=1$, the sonic point, at $r_s=\frac{5-3\gamma}{4}GM_{\rm BH}/c_\infty^2$. Not every solution passes through it; the figure below shows the whole family, and which one is physical.

For $\gamma=5/3$ the sonic point moves to $r\to0$.

The transonic accretion solution (the physical one) fixes the accretion radius and rate:

\begin{aligned}R_{\rm acc}&\simeq\frac{2GM_{\rm BH}}{c_s^2}\\ &\approx3.4\,\mathrm{pc}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^8M_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{c_s}{500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}\right)^{-2},\\ \dot M_B&=4\pi\lambda_\gamma\frac{G^2M_{\rm BH}^2\rho_\infty}{c_\infty^3}\\ &\approx1.6\times10^{-5}M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^8M_\odot}\right)^2\\ &\qquad\times\left(\frac{n_{\rm H}}{0.1\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}\right)\left(\frac{c_\infty}{500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}\right)^{-3}\end{aligned}

Here $\lambda_\gamma=1/4$ for $\gamma=5/3$. Hot gas around massive ellipticals therefore gives $\dot M_B\ll\dot M_{\rm Edd}$.

Thin disks. Gas that carries angular momentum cannot fall straight in: it must lose angular momentum to move inwards, and by the virial theorem half of the released potential energy must be dissipated, then radiated or advected. The standard model (Shakura and Sunyaev 1973) is a geometrically thin ($H/R\ll1$), optically thick, steady, Keplerian disk that radiates locally as a blackbody, with zero torque at $R_{\rm in}=r_{\rm ISCO}$. Angular momentum is carried outwards by an effective viscosity, $\nu=\alpha c_sH$ with $\alpha<1$ (physically, turbulence driven by the magnetorotational instability, $\alpha\sim0.01$ to $0.1$ in simulations). The surface temperature follows from the energy released at each radius:

\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4(R)=\frac{3GM_{\rm BH}\dot M}{8\pi R^3}\left[1-\left(\frac{R_{\rm in}}{R}\right)^{1/2}\right]

so $T\propto R^{-3/4}$ for $R\gg R_{\rm in}$, with a peak $T_{\max}\propto(\lambda_{\rm Edd}/M_{\rm BH})^{1/4}\approx7.5\times10^4$ K for $10^8M_\odot$ at $\lambda_{\rm Edd}=0.1$. AGN disks peak in the UV; X-ray binary disks, $\approx4\times10^6$ K for $10\,M_\odot$, peak in soft X-rays. Summing blackbody annuli gives the spectrum on the right: Rayleigh–Jeans ($F_\nu\propto\nu^2$) at low frequencies, $F_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}$ in between, and a Wien cut-off above $kT_{\max}$. The Newtonian disk luminosity is $L_{\rm disk}=GM_{\rm BH}\dot M/(2R_{\rm in})=\dot Mc^2/12$ for $R_{\rm in}=6\,r_g$, an efficiency of 8.3% against the relativistic 5.7%.

A thin disk: temperature profile, spectrum and timescales
\begin{aligned}t_{\rm dyn}&\simeq\Omega^{-1}=\left(\frac{R^3}{GM_{\rm BH}}\right)^{1/2},\\ t_{\rm th}&\simeq\frac{t_{\rm dyn}}{\alpha},\\ t_{\rm visc}&\simeq\frac{t_{\rm dyn}}{\alpha(H/R)^2}\end{aligned}

Since $\alpha<1$ and $H/R\ll1$, $t_{\rm dyn}\ll t_{\rm th}\ll t_{\rm visc}$: after a perturbation the disk first restores vertical hydrostatic equilibrium, then thermal equilibrium, and only then redistributes mass, which justifies the steady-state solution. Optical and UV variability on days to weeks is far faster than $t_{\rm visc}$, so it cannot reflect changes of the global accretion rate: it is attributed to thermal or dynamical processes, or to reprocessing of variable X-rays. A change in the fuel supply reaches the inner disk only after the viscous time of the outer disk, $\gg10^3$ yr, so witnessing an AGN switch off within decades should be improbable; changing-look AGN, which change type within years, challenge this picture.

Beyond the thin disk: the corona and other flows

A disk with $T\lesssim10^5$ K cannot make X-rays, yet $L_{2\text{–}10\,\rm keV}/L_{\rm bol}\sim1\text{–}10\%$. The answer is Comptonization: inverse Compton scattering of soft disk photons off hot electrons, each scattering raising the photon energy by $\langle\Delta E\rangle/E\simeq4kT_e/m_ec^2$ on average. The outcome depends on the Compton $y$ parameter:

\begin{aligned}y&=\frac{4kT_e}{m_ec^2}\max(\tau_e,\tau_e^2),\\ F_\nu&\propto\nu^{-\alpha},\\ \alpha&=-\frac32+\left(\frac94+\frac4y\right)^{1/2}\end{aligned}
From the Compton $y$ parameter to the photon index

For $y\gtrsim1$ repeated scatterings build a power law up to $\sim kT_e$ (Rybicki and Lightman 1979). A hot ($kT_e\sim100$ keV), moderately thin ($\tau_e\sim1$) plasma has $y\approx1$, hence $\alpha\approx1$ and the observed photon index $\Gamma=\alpha+1\approx2$. Hotter or thicker plasma gives a harder (flatter) spectrum. This postulated corona is compact (Sect. X-ray emission).

Other accretion regimes exist: slim disks at $\lambda_{\rm Edd}\gtrsim1$, where photons are trapped and advected and $\eta$ decreases, and advection-dominated (radiatively inefficient) flows at $\lambda_{\rm Edd}\lesssim0.01$, hot, geometrically thick and optically thin, which describe Sgr A$^*$, low-luminosity AGN and jet-mode AGN.

What we see: the AGN phenomenon

AGN were discovered as a zoo of apparently unrelated objects. A century of observations showed that one engine, seen through different amounts of gas and dust, from different angles and with or without a relativistic jet, explains most of the zoo.

A century of AGN

Three classes were recognised historically. Seyfert galaxies have a moderately luminous nucleus with the host galaxy clearly visible, mostly in spirals. Radio galaxies show jets and lobes and are mostly hosted by massive ellipticals. Quasars are the most luminous, with a point-like nucleus that outshines its host, and most are radio-quiet. The Seyfert/quasar boundary is one of luminosity, not of physics: a conventional threshold is $M_B<-23$ for quasars (Schmidt and Green 1983). Other classes are blazars (Sect. Jets) and LINERs (Sect. Narrow-line region). Click a milestone below.

The AGN zoo and the unified picture

Seyfert 1 galaxies show broad permitted lines (FWHM $\sim10^3$ to $10^4$ km/s) plus narrow permitted and forbidden lines (FWHM a few $10^2$ km/s); Seyfert 2 galaxies show narrow lines only (the distinction was introduced by Khachikian and Weedman in 1974). The key discovery came in 1985, when Antonucci and Miller found broad lines in the polarized, scattered light of the Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068: a hidden broad-line region, viewed directly in type 1 AGN and blocked in type 2. This is the basis of orientation-based unification. About 10 to 20% of AGN are radio-loud, $R=F_\nu(5\,{\rm GHz})/F_\nu(4400\,{\rm Å})>10$ (Kellermann et al. 1989); radio-quiet AGN can still host weak, small-scale jets. Padovani et al. (2017) recommend the terms jetted and non-jetted instead, since what matters is the presence of a strong relativistic jet rather than a radio-to-optical flux ratio.

Radiation, gas and dust: the ingredients

A few ideas from radiative transfer and atomic physics are used throughout. The transfer equation is $dI_\nu/ds=-\alpha_\nu I_\nu+j_\nu$; with $d\tau_\nu=\alpha_\nu ds$ and a constant source function $S_\nu=j_\nu/\alpha_\nu$ it has the formal solution

\begin{aligned}I_\nu(\tau_\nu)&=I_\nu(0)\,e^{-\tau_\nu}+S_\nu\left(1-e^{-\tau_\nu}\right),\\ \tau_\nu&=\int\alpha_\nu\,ds\end{aligned}
Radiation crossing a slab of gas

The incident intensity $I_\nu(0)$ is absorbed as $e^{-\tau_\nu}$, while the gas adds emission that pushes the intensity towards its source function $S_\nu$. After about one optical depth the memory of the background is gone and the slab shines as $S_\nu$, a blackbody for a thermal medium. A bright source behind the slab (top curve) is dimmed; a dark background (bottom curve) is brightened.

Pure absorption gives $I_\nu=I_\nu(0)e^{-\tau_\nu}$ (this is what the column density $N_{\rm H}$ does to the spectrum above), while an optically thick medium, $\tau_\nu\gg1$, gives $I_\nu\to S_\nu$ (a blackbody, for a thermal medium). The ionization structure of photoionized gas is set largely by the ionization parameter, the ratio of ionizing-photon density to hydrogen density:

\begin{aligned}U&=\frac{Q_{\rm ion}}{4\pi r^2 n_{\rm H}c},\\ Q_{\rm ion}&=\int_{\nu_0}^\infty\frac{L_\nu}{h\nu}\,d\nu,\qquad h\nu_0=13.6\ \mathrm{eV}\end{aligned}

Permitted and forbidden lines are defined by the selection rules, reflected in the Einstein coefficient $A_{ji}$: permitted (electric dipole) lines have $A_{ji}\sim10^6$ to $10^9\,{\rm s^{-1}}$, forbidden ones (magnetic dipole or electric quadrupole) $10^{-3}$ to $1\,{\rm s^{-1}}$, for example $A\approx0.02\,{\rm s^{-1}}$ for [O III] $\lambda5007$, and semi-forbidden or intercombination lines such as C III] $\lambda1909$ have $A\sim10^2\,{\rm s^{-1}}$. The critical density $n_{\rm crit}=A_{ji}/q_{ji}$, with $q_{ji}$ the collisional de-excitation rate coefficient, tells when collisions win: for $n_e\gg n_{\rm crit}$ the upper level is de-excited before it radiates and the line is suppressed. For [O III] $\lambda5007$, $n_{\rm crit}\approx7\times10^5\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$.

The default critical density is that of [O III] $\lambda5007$. The curve shows the fraction of collisional excitations that end as a photon; a permitted line is never suppressed.

Forbidden lines in the broad-line region are suppressed, not absorbed. And AGN line widths are kinematic: for hydrogen at $10^4$ K the thermal speed $v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2kT/m_{\rm H}}\approx13$ km/s is far below the observed widths. The Voigt profile (a Gaussian from Doppler broadening, thermal plus turbulent or bulk motions, convolved with a Lorentzian from natural and pressure broadening) is dominated by the Gaussian, so the line shape traces the gas motion.

Dust (see the Dust physics page) matters in several ways. It reprocesses radiation, absorbing UV and optical photons from stars and the AGN and re-emitting them thermally in the infrared; it cools gas through gas–grain collisions when $T_{\rm gas}>T_{\rm dust}$, efficient only in dense gas; it heats gas through the photoelectric effect, when FUV photons (6 to 13.6 eV) eject electrons from small grains and PAHs; it catalyses H$_2$ formation on grain surfaces and shields molecules from dissociating UV; it depletes refractory elements (Fe, Si, Mg) from the gas phase; and it causes extinction and reddening, measured for instance by the Balmer decrement H$\alpha$/H$\beta$, whose intrinsic value is $\approx2.86$ for Case B at $10^4$ K and $\approx3.1$ often adopted for AGN narrow-line regions.

X-ray emission

X-ray emission, from about 0.1 to 200 keV, is nearly universal in AGN and the most complete selection method. The exception is Compton-thick sources, with $N_{\rm H}\gtrsim\sigma_T^{-1}\approx1.5\times10^{24}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$, where only hard X-rays ($\gtrsim10$ keV), reflected or scattered, escape. Photoelectric absorption by intervening gas suppresses the soft X-rays and measures the line-of-sight column density; type 2 AGN typically have $N_{\rm H}>10^{22}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$ (try the line-of-sight slider in the spectrum above). Three components are distinguished:

(i) Primary continuum
A power law $F_E\propto E^{-\Gamma}$ with $\Gamma=\alpha+1\approx1.8$ to $2$, from inverse Compton scattering of disk photons by hot electrons in the corona ($kT_e\sim50$ to $100$ keV, $\tau_e\sim1$), with an exponential cut-off at $E_c\approx2$ to $3\,kT_e\sim100$ to $300$ keV.
(ii) Reflection
Primary X-rays irradiating the disk or torus produce the Fe K$\alpha$ fluorescence line at 6.4 keV (6.7 and 6.97 keV for Fe XXV and Fe XXVI) and a Compton hump peaking at $\sim20$ to $30$ keV, shaped by photoelectric absorption at low energies and Compton recoil at high energies. The line and the hump are distinct features.
(iii) Soft excess
Emission below $\sim1$ to $2$ keV whose origin is debated: warm, optically thick Comptonization ($kT_e\sim0.1$ to $1$ keV) or relativistically blurred ionized reflection.

Not to be confused with the intracluster medium: cluster X-rays are thermal bremsstrahlung plus line emission from diffuse, optically thin plasma at $kT\sim1$ to $10$ keV, with no Comptonization of an external seed-photon field. The corona is compact, $\sim3$ to $10\,r_g$, from X-ray reverberation lags and quasar microlensing; its origin is unclear (magnetic heating through reconnection of buoyant disk fields, or the base of a failed jet).

Fe K$\alpha$ as a probe of strong gravity. For a line emitted at 6.4 keV by a rotating disk, each annulus gives a symmetric double-peaked profile from the approaching and receding sides (Newtonian). Special relativity then boosts the blue peak (Doppler beaming) and the transverse Doppler shift moves the whole profile to lower energies. General relativity adds the gravitational redshift near the black hole, producing an extended red wing. The emission extends down to $\sim r_{\rm ISCO}$, which depends on the spin, so the red wing constrains the spin and whether the disk is prograde or retrograde, and the blue edge constrains the inclination (prototype: MCG$-$6-30-15, Tanaka et al. 1995). Reflection probes the inner accretion disk; the power law probes the corona.

The broad-line region and reverberation mapping

Broad permitted lines (Ly$\alpha$, C IV $\lambda1549$, Mg II $\lambda2798$, H$\beta$, H$\alpha$) with FWHM $\sim10^3$ to $10^4$ km/s form close to the black hole, in dense gas ($n_e\sim10^9$ to $10^{11}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$) whose density exceeds the critical densities of forbidden transitions. Ratios of lines cancel unknowns such as distance, geometry and covering factor to first order, and constrain the ionization parameter and the density. Line fluxes respond to continuum variations with a lag $\tau\approx R_{\rm BLR}/c$, the light-travel time from the central source to the gas: reverberation mapping measures this lag. $R_{\rm BLR}$ is a few to tens of light-days in Seyferts and hundreds in luminous quasars. High-ionization lines (He II, C IV) have shorter lags and larger widths than low-ionization ones (H$\beta$, Mg II), consistent with virial motion $v\propto R^{-1/2}$ (Peterson and Wandel 1999).

The continuum flickers, the gas answers later interactive

Clouds light up when the continuum pulse reaches them, and we receive their light later by an amount that depends on where they sit relative to us. A shell of clouds smears the response over $0$ to $2R/c$, and the mean delay is $R/c$. The continuum is a random walk; the simulation runs faster than real time.

A second ingredient makes this a mass measurement. Combining the radius with the line width, the virial theorem gives the black hole mass, and the empirical radius–luminosity relation lets a single spectrum replace a monitoring campaign (single-epoch masses):

\begin{aligned}M_{\rm BH}&=f\,\frac{R_{\rm BLR}\,(\Delta v)^2}{G},\\ R_{\rm BLR}(\mathrm{H}\beta)&\propto L_{5100}^{0.53}\end{aligned}

Here $R_{\rm BLR}=c\tau$ from reverberation mapping, or from the $R$–$L$ relation using a single spectrum; $\Delta v$ is the FWHM or the line dispersion $\sigma_{\rm line}$; and $f$ absorbs the unknown geometry, kinematics and inclination, calibrated by matching reverberation-mapped AGN to the $M_{\rm BH}$–$\sigma_*$ relation ($\langle f\rangle\approx4$ to $5$ for $\sigma_{\rm line}$). Single-epoch masses use H$\beta$ for $z\lesssim0.8$, Mg II for $0.4\lesssim z\lesssim2.2$ and C IV for $z\gtrsim1.5$, the latter affected by outflow-driven blueshifts; the systematic uncertainty is $\sim0.3$ to $0.5$ dex. The $R$–$L$ relation is observed ($R\approx34$ light-days at $\lambda L_\lambda(5100\,{\rm Å})=10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}$, Bentz et al. 2013) and follows from similar ionization parameter and density across luminosity: $U\propto L/(R^2n_{\rm H})$ gives $R\propto L^{1/2}$. AGN with high accretion rates show shorter lags than predicted (Du et al. 2016, 2018). The mass of line-emitting gas is tiny ($10^{-3}$ to $1M_\odot$) and the volume filling factor $\epsilon\ll1$, so the gas is clumpy or filamentary, with a covering factor of about $0.1$.

The picture is an ensemble of clouds gravitationally bound to, and orbiting, the black hole. Reverberation with $\Delta v^2c\tau/G$ and the $R$–$L$ relation are the main tools for black hole masses beyond the local Universe. Velocity-resolved reverberation mapping and near-infrared interferometry (GRAVITY on 3C 273) favour a rotating, geometrically thick, disk-like BLR, with inflow or outflow components in some objects.

The dusty obscurer

Near 1 µm the declining big blue bump and the rising dust emission cross, producing a minimum in $\nu F_\nu$; longward of it AGN-heated dust dominates. Grains are destroyed above $T_{\rm sub}\approx1500$ K, which defines the sublimation radius, the inner edge of the dusty structure and roughly the outer edge of the BLR:

\begin{aligned}R_{\rm sub}&\approx1.3\,\mathrm{pc}\left(\frac{L_{\rm UV}}{10^{46}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)^{1/2}\\ &\qquad\times\left(\frac{T_{\rm sub}}{1500\,\mathrm{K}}\right)^{-2.8}\end{aligned}

(Barvainis 1987), i.e. $\sim0.1$ pc for Seyferts and $\sim1$ pc for luminous quasars. Hot dust at $R_{\rm sub}$ emits in the near-infrared, at 2 to 3 µm, and near-infrared reverberation lags follow $R\propto L^{1/2}$; cooler dust at larger radii emits in the mid-infrared, at $\sim3$ to $30$ µm. The 9.7 µm silicate feature appears in absorption in type 2 (edge-on) AGN and weakly in emission in type 1.

The dusty structure can block the line of sight to the accretion disk and the BLR, hiding the broad lines: type 2. The evidence is polarized broad lines (Antonucci and Miller 1985), large X-ray columns and ionization cones. Mid-infrared interferometry places most warm dust at $\sim0.1$ to $10$ pc; ALMA resolves a molecular torus of 7 to 10 pc diameter in NGC 1068 (García-Burillo et al. 2016), the associated molecular gas extending to $\sim100$ pc. 22 GHz H$_2$O water masers trace thin sub-parsec disks roughly co-spatial with the inner dusty and molecular disk: VLBI positions plus line-of-sight velocities give a Keplerian rotation curve, hence $M_{\rm BH}\approx4\times10^7M_\odot$ and a geometric distance of 7.6 Mpc for NGC 4258 (Humphreys et al. 2013).

Clumpiness. Smooth dust distributions predict strong silicate emission in type 1s and a narrow temperature range at each radius. The observed weak features and broad temperature range, from directly and indirectly illuminated cloud faces, favour a clumpy distribution; the temperature spread is a consequence of clumpiness, not its cause. Variable X-ray columns from clouds crossing the line of sight point the same way (Risaliti et al. 2005). Polar dust. Mid-infrared interferometry shows much of the emission elongated along the polar axis (Hönig et al. 2012, 2013), interpreted as dusty winds.

The obscurer is not simply a flat disk or a doughnut. The current picture (Ramos Almeida and Ricci 2017; Hönig 2019), from small to large scales, is a geometrically thin inner disk (hot dust near $R_{\rm sub}$, an H$_2$O maser disk at sub-pc scales), a thicker molecular and dusty structure at pc scales traced by CO and HCN with ALMA, and a hollow-cone dusty wind along the polar direction connecting to larger-scale molecular outflows. The equatorial disk obscures at high inclinations and the polar wind possibly at intermediate ones. Try both in the anatomy figure above.

The narrow-line region and the BPT diagram

The narrow-line region (NLR) extends from $\sim10$ pc to $\sim1$ kpc, with extended NLRs reaching $\gtrsim10$ kpc, well beyond the galaxy centre. Lines are narrow (FWHM a few $10^2$ km/s) and the electron density is low, $n_e\sim10^2$ to $10^4\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$, below the critical densities of the strong forbidden lines, which are therefore prominent. The NLR often forms ionization cones with opening angles of $30^\circ$ to $100^\circ$, shaped by the nuclear obscurer: the ionizing radiation field is anisotropic. The gas is clumpy ($\epsilon\lesssim10^{-2}$) and photoionized by the AGN. Forbidden lines are collisionally excited by thermal electrons, heated by photoionization, and are the main coolants; Balmer lines arise from recombination. Diagnostics: $n_e$ from [S II] $\lambda6716/\lambda6731$ and $T_e$ from [O III] $(\lambda4959+\lambda5007)/\lambda4363$.

The BPT diagram (Baldwin, Phillips and Terlevich 1981), [O III] $\lambda5007$/H$\beta$ against [N II] $\lambda6584$/H$\alpha$, separates ionization sources. H II regions, with soft stellar spectra, lie at lower left. AGN lie at upper right: their harder ionizing spectrum, including X-rays, penetrates deeper and creates an extended, partially ionized zone where low-ionization lines ([N II], [S II], [O I]) are strong while [O III]/H$\beta$ stays high. The demarcation lines are the theoretical maximum starburst (Kewley et al. 2001) and an empirical one (Kauffmann et al. 2003); composites lie between. Diagrams with [S II]/H$\alpha$ and [O I]/H$\alpha$ (Veilleux and Osterbrock 1987) separate Seyferts from LINERs (Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions, Heckman 1980), whose ionization source is debated: low-luminosity AGN with radiatively inefficient accretion, shocks, or hot evolved (post-AGB) stars, the latter favoured for spatially extended LINER-like emission. Each ratio uses lines close in wavelength (H$\beta$ 4861 Å with [O III] 5007 Å, H$\alpha$ 6563 Å with [N II] 6584 Å), so the diagram is nearly insensitive to dust reddening and flux calibration.

$L_{\rm [O\,III]}$ correlates with the hard X-ray luminosity and serves as a proxy for AGN power, since the NLR lies outside the obscurer: $L_{\rm bol}\approx3500\,L_{\rm [O\,III]}$ without extinction correction (Heckman et al. 2004), with the caveats of extinction and contamination from star formation.

Jets and radio galaxies

The Fanaroff–Riley classes (Fanaroff and Riley 1974) split radio galaxies by morphology. FR I sources have lower radio luminosity and edge-darkened lobes, brightest near the core, and their jets decelerate on kiloparsec scales. FR II sources have higher radio luminosity and edge-brightened lobes with compact hotspots where the jets terminate in shocks; the FR I/II dividing luminosity increases with the host's optical luminosity (Owen and Ledlow 1994). Radio emission is synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons, $S_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha\approx0.7$ where optically thin; compact cores have flat spectra owing to synchrotron self-absorption.

Beaming. Bulk Lorentz factors $\Gamma\sim10$ produce apparent superluminal motion, $\beta_{\rm app}=\beta\sin\theta/(1-\beta\cos\theta)$, and Doppler boosting. Blazars are jets viewed close to the line of sight: BL Lacs and FSRQs are the beamed counterparts of FR I and FR II, respectively. Their spectra show two broad humps (inset of the spectrum above, in the blazar view): synchrotron emission from radio to UV or X-rays, and inverse Compton emission from X-rays to $\gamma$-rays. Blazars are classified by the synchrotron peak frequency (Abdo et al. 2010): LSP (low, $\nu_S<10^{14}$ Hz), ISP ($10^{14}$ to $10^{15}$ Hz) and HSP (high, $\nu_S>10^{15}$ Hz). FSRQs are almost all LSP; BL Lacs span all three. The seed photons for the inverse Compton hump are either the jet's own synchrotron photons (SSC, typical of HSP) or external photons from the BLR, the torus or the disk (external Compton, typical of Compton-dominated LSP sources such as FSRQs). The blazar sequence (Fossati et al. 1998; Ghisellini et al. 1998) says that more luminous blazars have lower $\nu_S$ and larger Compton dominance; whether it is intrinsic or partly a selection effect is debated (Giommi et al. 2012).

Apparent speed and Doppler boosting of a jet blob interactive

The apparent speed peaks near $\theta\approx1/\Gamma$. The Doppler factor is $\delta=1/[\Gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)]$ and the flux of a moving blob is boosted by about $\delta^3$ to $\delta^4$.

Launching. Jets are powered either by extraction of the black hole's spin energy through horizon-threading magnetic fields (Blandford and Znajek 1977) or by magnetocentrifugal acceleration from the disk (Blandford and Payne 1982). Jet-mode AGN typically accrete at $\lambda_{\rm Edd}\lesssim0.01$, in a radiatively inefficient regime (Heckman and Best 2014).

Hosts, growth and cosmic history

A black hole a thousandth the mass of its bulge should barely notice its host, yet the two are tightly correlated. This chapter looks at that link, at how AGN activity evolved with cosmic time, and at the problem of growing billion-solar-mass black holes within a billion years.

Black holes and their host galaxies

$M_{\rm BH}$ correlates with properties of the host: with the stellar velocity dispersion, $M_{\rm BH}\propto\sigma_*^{4\text{–}5}$ (Ferrarese and Merritt 2000; Gebhardt et al. 2000), and with the bulge stellar mass, $M_{\rm BH}/M_{\rm bulge}\approx0.5\%$ for ellipticals and classical bulges (Kormendy and Ho 2013). The correlations are weak or absent for pseudobulges and bulgeless disks. They are commonly read as co-evolution, but the mechanism is not established: proposed explanations are self-regulation by AGN feedback (Peng 2007) and a common gas supply, or statistical convergence through hierarchical mergers (Jahnke and Macciò 2011).

Feedback comes in two modes (Fabian 2012). The radiative or quasar mode occurs at high $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$ and drives winds and outflows. The kinetic or jet mode occurs at low $\lambda_{\rm Edd}$: in cool-core clusters, jets inflate X-ray cavities with enthalpies $4PV\sim10^{55}$ to $10^{62}$ erg, and jet powers are comparable to the cooling luminosity of the intracluster medium in many systems (McNamara and Nulsen 2007).

Cosmic evolution

Surveys drive this field. SDSS is a multi-purpose imaging and spectroscopic survey, not only a quasar survey, but the source of the largest spectroscopic quasar catalogues (DR16Q: $\approx7.5\times10^5$ quasars; Lyke et al. 2020). eROSITA, on the SRG satellite, made the first all-sky X-ray survey in decades; its first all-sky pass (eRASS1) alone contains $\sim9\times10^5$ sources, mostly AGN (Merloni et al. 2024). 4MOST on VISTA has dedicated AGN surveys including eROSITA follow-up, DESI observes spectroscopic quasars, and Euclid and JWST probe faint and high-redshift AGN.

Surveys give both the cosmic star formation rate density and the black hole accretion rate density versus redshift. Both peak at $z\approx2$ and decline by about an order of magnitude to $z=0$ (Madau and Dickinson 2014). The parallel histories are consistent with co-evolution without demonstrating a causal link. The Sołtan argument (Sołtan 1982) adds that the mass accreted, inferred from the integrated AGN light, matches the local SMBH mass density for $\eta\approx0.1$: supermassive black holes grew mostly through radiatively efficient accretion (Yu and Tremaine 2002; Marconi et al. 2004).

Growing monsters: Salpeter time and seeds

With $\dot M_{\rm BH}=(1-\eta)\dot M$ and $L=\eta\dot Mc^2=\lambda_{\rm Edd}L_{\rm Edd}$, Eddington-limited growth is exponential, with the Salpeter time:

\begin{aligned}M_{\rm BH}(t)&=M_0\exp\left(\frac{t}{t_S}\right),\\ t_S&=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,\frac{\sigma_Tc}{4\pi Gm_p}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm Edd}}\\ &\approx50\ \mathrm{Myr}\quad(\eta=0.1,\ \lambda_{\rm Edd}=1)\end{aligned}

This leads to a timing problem. Quasars with $M_{\rm BH}\sim10^9M_\odot$ exist at $z>7$: J0313$-$1806 at $z=7.64$ has $1.6\times10^9M_\odot$ (Wang et al. 2021), only about $0.7$ Gyr after the Big Bang. Growing from $10^2M_\odot$ requires $\ln(1.6\times10^7)\approx17$ e-folds, about $0.8$ Gyr of uninterrupted Eddington-limited accretion. JWST detections of AGN at $z\gtrsim10$, such as UHZ1 (Bogdán et al. 2024), sharpen the problem.

Light seeds
Remnants of Population III stars, $\sim10^2M_\odot$. Insufficient under standard Eddington-limited growth; radiative feedback and low ambient densities further limit accretion.
Direct collapse, heavy seeds
In metal-free, atomic-cooling halos ($T_{\rm vir}\gtrsim10^4$ K), Lyman–Werner photons (11.2 to 13.6 eV) from nearby star-forming halos dissociate H$_2$. The gas stays at $\sim8000$ K; the high Jeans mass and inflow rates suppress fragmentation and form a single supermassive star of $\sim10^5M_\odot$, which collapses through a general-relativistic instability after a short lifetime. Seeds of $\sim10^4$ to $10^6M_\odot$; this requires finely tuned conditions (strong Lyman–Werner flux, no metals).
Stellar collisions, intermediate seeds
Runaway collisions in dense nuclear star clusters build a very massive star, leaving seeds of $\sim10^3M_\odot$. Primordial black holes, formed from early-Universe density fluctuations, are not mainstream but still discussed.

Super-Eddington phases, $\lambda_{\rm Edd}>1$, for example by photon trapping in slim disks, can also relax the timing problem for light seeds.

Little red dots

Little red dots (LRDs) are compact, red sources discovered by JWST, mostly at $z\sim4$ to $8$. They are one to two orders of magnitude more numerous than UV-selected quasars of similar luminosity (Matthee et al. 2024; Greene et al. 2024), and their number density appears to drop at lower redshift. Their observed properties are unlike those of ordinary AGN: a V-shaped continuum (blue rest-UV, red rest-optical, with the inflection near the Balmer limit, $\simeq3650$ Å, and often a strong Balmer break); broad Balmer lines in $\gtrsim80\%$ of spectroscopic samples, often with narrow Balmer absorption on top and a steep Balmer decrement, $\mathrm{H}\alpha/\mathrm{H}\beta\gtrsim10$; and X-ray weakness, no hot-dust emission in the rest-frame near and mid-infrared, no cold dust in the far-infrared, no radio detection and little variability.

The first interpretations, dust-reddened AGN or compact massive galaxies, cannot explain these properties together: dust reddening predicts infrared emission that is not seen, and stellar populations cannot produce the deepest Balmer breaks. The black hole star model (Inayoshi and Maiolino 2025; Naidu et al. 2025; de Graaff et al. 2025) places an accreting black hole inside dense gas ($n_{\rm H}\sim10^9$ to $10^{11}\,{\rm cm^{-3}}$, $N_{\rm H}\sim10^{24}$ to $10^{26}\,{\rm cm^{-2}}$) with a covering fraction close to unity. Hydrogen collisionally excited to $n=2$ produces the Balmer break and absorption; the red continuum is a photosphere with $T_{\rm eff}\sim4000$ to $5000$ K, not dust reddening. In a stack of 98 LRDs it supplies $\sim20\%$ of the UV, $\sim50\%$ of the light at the Balmer break and $\sim90\%$ longward of H$\alpha$; the rest comes from a low-mass ($M_*\sim10^8M_\odot$), starbursting host (Sun et al. 2026).

Their nature remains debated. Black hole masses are uncertain: if electron scattering in the dense gas broadens the line wings, single-epoch virial masses (Sect. Broad-line region) are overestimated (Rusakov et al. 2026). LRDs may trace an early, possibly super-Eddington growth phase of black holes, relevant to the seed problem above; compact star clusters and supermassive stars have also been proposed.
