Cosmic dust

Cosmic dust is made of solid grains from about a nanometre to a micrometre across, mostly silicates, carbon-rich material and ices, together with large molecules called PAHs. It makes up only about 1% of the mass of the interstellar medium, but it matters far more than that: it absorbs ultraviolet and optical light and re-emits it in the infrared, so it shapes almost everything JWST sees. It locks up heavy elements, heats the gas through the photoelectric effect, cools gas so that clouds can collapse into stars, and forms molecular hydrogen on its surface. It is also the raw material of planets.

This page covers where dust comes from, the main kinds of grains, and the processes that build and destroy them. The equations and parameters follow the CALIMA dust model for galaxy simulations (Rodríguez Montero et al. 2026), unless stated otherwise. Grain features such as the PAH bands and the silicate absorption are the ones tracked in the MIRI feature map.

How dust forms

Dust grains form by condensation: in a gas that is cool and dense enough, atoms and molecules of refractory elements (Mg, Si, Fe, C, Al, O) stick together into clusters, which then grow. Condensation needs the gas to be supersaturated,

where p_i is the partial pressure of a species and p_sat its saturation vapour pressure at the gas temperature T. In practice this happens in gas that has cooled below about 1000 to 1800 K, depending on the material, which limits the places where grains are born to a few kinds of sources:

Evolved low-mass stars (AGB)
The cool, dense winds of asymptotic giant branch stars (1 to 8 solar masses) cool as they expand, and dust condenses within a few stellar radii. The C/O ratio decides what forms: CO locks up all the carbon or oxygen it can, so the leftover element makes the dust. Oxygen-rich stars (C/O < 1) make silicates; carbon-rich ones (C/O ≥ 1) make carbon grains and, according to the paper, some PAHs.
Core-collapse supernovae
The ejecta of massive-star explosions cool quickly and are rich in refractory elements, so grains condense there. Supernova dust is observed to be dominated by relatively large grains (0.1 µm and above), which survive the reverse shock better, so the model puts all of it in the large-grain population. Type Ia supernovae are assumed to make no dust. The condensation efficiencies are uncertain by up to an order of magnitude.
Growth in the interstellar medium
In dense, cold gas, metals from the gas phase stick onto existing grains (accretion) and grains stick to each other (coagulation). Stellar sources dominate dust production at low metallicity, while growth in the ISM takes over in metal-rich galaxies, which is why the dust-to-metal ratio rises with metallicity.

The kinds of dust

Dust is not one substance but a mixture of populations that differ in size, composition and temperature. The CALIMA model describes it with a two-size, two-composition scheme, plus PAHs as separate molecules: small and large amorphous carbonaceous grains, small and large silicate grains, and small and large PAHs. What dust looks like up close

Dust grains come in a few basic structures, sketched below (not to scale). A grain in dense, cold gas has a refractory core, either a silicate (the model uses an olivine, MgFeSiO4, whose Si–O bonds produce the 9.7 and 18 µm features) or amorphous carbon, wrapped in layers that depend on where it has been: a thin carbon-rich organic mantle made by ultraviolet processing, and, where the gas is cold and dense enough, an ice mantle of water, CO and other molecules. PAHs are not grains at all but individual molecules, which can clump into larger stacks, and grains themselves can stick together into aggregates.

How dust dims starlight: the extinction curve

Dust absorbs and scatters light, and grains of radius a are most efficient at wavelengths lambda with 2 pi a / lambda ~ 1, so small grains dim the ultraviolet and large grains the optical. Distant stars therefore look both fainter and redder. The extinction A_lambda (in magnitudes, relative to the V band) as a function of inverse wavelength is the fingerprint of the grain population: the bump at 2175 Å comes from small carbon grains and PAHs, and the steepness of the curve depends on the grain sizes through R_V, about 3.1 in the diffuse Milky Way and larger in dense clouds, where grains have grown. Matching the near-infrared and optical slope of the Milky Way curve is one of the constraints that fixes the large grains at about 0.1 µm with a sharp cut-off at larger sizes. The curve here is the empirical Cardelli, Clayton & Mathis (1989) fit.

R_V is the ratio of the total dimming in the V band to the reddening, the difference in dimming between the B and V bands: it tells you how much a given amount of reddening costs in lost light. It is a measure of how grey the dust is. A low R_V (about 2 to 3) means small grains dominate: the ultraviolet is dimmed much more than the optical, the curve is steep, and the light is strongly reddened. A high R_V (about 4 to 6) means the grains have grown larger, as in dense clouds: the curve is flatter, all wavelengths are dimmed more evenly, and the light is reddened less for the same total dimming. Destruction in supernova shocks

A supernova blast wave destroys the dust it sweeps over, through non-thermal (inertial) sputtering and grain-grain collisions. This does not depend on the temperature of the gas but on the kinetic energy of the shock. Small grains are decelerated by drag and stay near the shock, where hot gas sputters them quickly, while large grains keep moving through it, so small grains are destroyed more efficiently. Only dust that was already in the ISM is destroyed: the dust newly formed in the supernova ejecta is not.

Shattering and coagulation

Turbulence makes grains collide at speeds that depend on their sizes and on the Mach number of the gas. What happens next depends on the speed of the collision.

Shattering: fast collisions

PAHs: a molecular life cycle
PAHs follow their own rules. Small PAHs collide and cluster into large ones, large PAHs freeze onto bigger grains, UV photons dissociate them, and hot ions sputter them. Dust and the gas: heating and H2 formation

Dust is not only shaped by its surroundings: it also controls the thermal and chemical state of the gas. Two effects matter most in the CALIMA model, and both depend on the grain size distribution, so the processes above feed back on them.

Photoelectric heating

A far-ultraviolet photon (below the 13.6 eV hydrogen ionisation limit) absorbed by a grain or a PAH can eject an electron. If the electron reaches the surface with more energy than the work function of the material and than the electrostatic pull of the grain’s charge, it escapes and shares its energy with the gas. This photoelectric heating is the dominant heating source of neutral diffuse gas at roughly solar metallicity, and it is balanced mainly by cooling in the [C II] and [O I] fine-structure lines. Small grains heat the gas best, because their electrons are close to the surface and the grains stay nearly neutral, while large grains get positively charged and hold on to their electrons. Most of the heating therefore comes from the small-grain end of the size distribution, including PAHs, so shattering, coagulation and PAH destruction all change how the gas is heated.

Molecular hydrogen formation

Two hydrogen atoms that meet in the gas cannot easily bind into H2, because there is nothing to carry off the energy released. A grain surface solves this: the atoms stick, find each other, bind, and the grain absorbs the energy. This catalysis is how nearly all molecular hydrogen in the interstellar medium forms. The paper follows two routes on grains: atoms hopping between weakly bound (physisorbed) sites until they meet, which stops working once the grain is warmer than about 20 K because the atoms evaporate, and atoms that react from strongly bound (chemisorbed) sites, which still works on warm grains.

The Milky Way value is about 20%.

The paper finds the same trend in its simulated galaxies: molecular hydrogen forms much faster where the small-to-large grain ratio is high, and more slowly where the dust is warm. In most of the simulated gas the formation coefficient is far below the canonical Milky Way value of 3.5 × 10−17 cm3 s−1, and only approaches it in shielded dense clumps, where there is a lot of small dust that stays cold.
