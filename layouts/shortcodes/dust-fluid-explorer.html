Dust Fluid in AthenaPK

Grains in hot gas are not inert. They are eroded by sputtering, they grow by accreting metals out of the gas, and their collisions with electrons radiate away the thermal energy of the gas. Which of these wins depends on the temperature and the density, and on the size of the grain. AthenaPK therefore does not carry a single dust density: it carries a size-resolved dust population in every cell, as a passive fluid that moves with the gas and exchanges only energy with it.

This page explains how the module works and why it is built this way: what a cell stores, how the grain-size distribution is reconstructed inside a bin and moved from bin to bin as grains grow or shrink, how the dust cools the gas, how all of it is tied into the time step, and how dust enters the box. The model is that of McKinnon et al. (2018) for a hydrodynamically passive dust population, with a log-linear reconstruction added. The AthenaPK module was written by Fred Jennings (UNIMORE) within the ERC project BlackHoleWeather; its documentation asks users to contact the author before using it.

A ✦ marks a section with an interactive figure. Most figures use the formulas of the module, re-implemented in this page, and the text says so where it matters. The figures of the testing chapter are different: they are output of AthenaPK runs of the repository's test inputs, made for this page.

The dust fluid

The dust is a set of passive scalars on the mesh. Their meaning, and the reason there are two per size bin, are the foundation of everything that follows.

What a cell stores

A grain population is described by its size distribution $\mathrm dn/\mathrm da$, the number of grains per unit volume and per unit grain radius $a$. The module splits the range between grainsize_bins_low_edge and grainsize_bins_high_edge into num_grainsize_bins bins that are equally spaced in $\log a$, separately for each composition (carbonaceous, silicate, or both). In each bin $i$ a cell carries two numbers:

n_i=\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm da}\,\mathrm da,\qquad \rho_{\mathrm{d},i}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\rho_{\rm grain}\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}a^3\,\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm da}\,\mathrm da

the grain number density and the grain mass density, with $\rho_{\rm grain}$ the material density of the composition (for example 2.2 and $3.3\ {\rm g\,cm^{-3}}$). They are stored as passive scalars: in the conserved variables they are densities, in the primitive ones they are divided by the gas density, and the hydrodynamic solver advects them with the gas like any other species. The scalars are laid out composition by composition, carbonaceous first, then silicate, and within a composition bin by bin with the number density first and the mass density next. The first one sits at dust_scalar_idx_start.

Two consequences of this design set the character of the module. There is no drag and no back-reaction: the grains follow the gas exactly and act on it only through the cooling. And because the scalars are conserved densities, their total is preserved by advection and changes only through the source terms below.

Why two numbers per bin

Many quantities are integrals over the size distribution, and a bin average alone does not give them. To do better, the module reconstructs the shape of $\mathrm dn/\mathrm da$ inside each bin from the two numbers it has, $n_i$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}$. The ratio of the two fixes one number, the mean grain mass of the bin, and so one free parameter of the shape. Three reconstructions exist, chosen with piecewise_method.

  • linear (McKinnon et al. 2018, eq. 31) is a straight line $\mathrm dn/\mathrm da=n_i/(a_{i+1}-a_i)+s_i\,(a-a_{\rm M})$ about the bin midpoint $a_{\rm M}$. The slope $s_i$ is solved from the mass. Where the line would go negative at an edge, slope limiting (eqs. 40 to 43) sets it to zero there and keeps the mass, which changes the grain number.
  • loglinear (new in this implementation) is a power law $\mathrm dn/\mathrm da=\beta\,a^{\kappa}$, with $\beta$ from the number and $\kappa$ from the mass by a Newton iteration. A power law is positive everywhere, and it is what an MRN distribution is inside any bin. If no $\kappa$ with $|\kappa|\le45$ reproduces both numbers, for instance when all grains sit near one edge, the mass is kept and the number adjusted.
  • hybrid_loglinear treats such a bin as a $\delta$ function at the mean grain size, $a_\delta=\left[3\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}/(4\pi\rho_{\rm grain}n_i)\right]^{1/3}$, which keeps both numbers.

A limit worth knowing: with few, wide bins nothing fixes how well the shape is captured, and a distribution that is steep across a bin (small grains abundant, large ones rare) is exactly what the linear form cannot represent. This is why the log-linear mode was added.

Growth and destruction of grains

Two processes change the size of a grain: accretion of gas-phase metals makes it grow, thermal sputtering by hot ions makes it shrink. The module gives both as a rate $\dot a=\mathrm da/\mathrm dt$ that does not depend on the size of the grain, and that single fact makes the bookkeeping of the bins tractable.

Accretion and sputtering

Sputtering follows Tsai and Mathews (1995), in the form of McKinnon et al. (2018, eq. 50). With $n=\rho_{\rm gas}/m_{\rm H}$ the number density of gas nuclei,

\dot a_{\rm sput}=-f_{\rm sput}\;3.2\times10^{-18}\ {\rm cm^4\,s^{-1}}\;\frac{n}{1+(T_0/T)^{2.5}},\qquad T_0=2\times10^{6}\ {\rm K}

where $f_{\rm sput}$ is sputtering_suppresion_factor (default 1). Below $T_0$ the rate collapses as $T^{2.5}$: sputtering is a hot-gas process. Accretion follows Hirashita and Kuo (2011, McKinnon et al. eq. 49), with the hydrogen density $n_{\rm H}$,

\dot a_{\rm acc}=\frac{Z}{\mathrm{Z}_\odot}\left(\frac{n_{\rm H}}{10^{3}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}}\right)\left(\frac{T}{10\ {\rm K}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{S}{0.3}\ \ \mu{\rm m\ Gyr^{-1}}

with the sticking efficiency $S=0.3$ and the metallicity fixed at $Z=0.33\,{\rm Z}_\odot$. The reservoir of gas-phase metals is not depleted by the growth, so accretion cannot run out; this is one of the simplifications the documentation lists for the future. The two rates switch on separately (thermal_sputtering, metal_accretion) and add up to $\dot a$. Both are evaluated in each cell from the local temperature and density. A positive sputtering rate, or a negative accretion rate, is unphysical: in the interior it stops the run, and in ghost cells it is set to zero.

In the hot, thin gas of a cluster core sputtering wins; in the cold, dense gas of filaments accretion does. A cell that cools from one regime to the other inside a single time step is the reason the time integration of the next chapter exists.

Moving grains between bins

Because $\dot a$ is the same for every grain size, a time step $\Delta t$ moves every grain by the same $\Delta=\dot a\,\Delta t$: a grain of radius $a$ ends at $a+\Delta$. The new contents of bin $j$ then come from the parts of the old bins $i$ that land inside it. Grains that start in the interval $[x_1,x_2]$ of bin $i$ end in bin $j$ with

x_1=\max(a_i^{\rm L},\,a_j^{\rm L}-\Delta),\qquad x_2=\min(a_i^{\rm U},\,a_j^{\rm U}-\Delta)

(McKinnon et al. eqs. 32 and 33). Bin $i$ contributes only if $x_2>x_1$. What it contributes is an integral of the reconstructed distribution over $[x_1,x_2]$, in number and in mass, with the mass counted at the grown size,

\delta n_{i\to j}=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm da}\,\mathrm da,\qquad \delta\rho_{\mathrm{d},i\to j}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_{\rm grain}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}(a+\Delta)^3\,\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm da}\,\mathrm da

and the new $n_j$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{d},j}$ are the sums over all $i$. Closed forms exist for both reconstructions; they involve differences of powers that lose precision when an exponent is close to zero, so the code evaluates them with a Taylor series about the bin midpoint when needed. Three details close the scheme. A ghost bin sits above the largest bin: grains that grow past $a_{\max}$ end there, and their mass is moved to the top bin as grains of radius exactly $a_{\max}$ (eqs. 44 to 46). Grains that are sputtered below the smallest edge simply leave the grid: they are destroyed. And the update never makes a density negative.

Three things show up when you play with it. The error falls as the bins get narrower. For an MRN distribution the log-linear reconstruction is exact to rounding error at any number of bins, because an MRN distribution is a power law inside every bin, while the linear one converges roughly as the square of the bin width. A flat distribution is exact for both, since a flat line is also a power law with $\kappa=0$. For a distribution that is not a power law inside a bin, such as the AGB-like one, none of the three is exact; the log-linear form is still the closer, by a factor of a few. And the shift acts on the distribution as a whole, so a shift larger than a bin pushes grains over several bins in a single step; the scheme handles that without any change, since each bin contributes to every bin its shifted interval overlaps.

Dust and the gas

The grains reach the gas in one way only, by cooling it. This chapter gives that cooling rate, three ways of evaluating it, and how the grain update is tied to the cooling subcycles.

Cooling by grain–electron collisions

In gas hot enough to be ionized, electrons hit grains, deposit energy in them, and the grain re-radiates it in the infrared. The module uses the heating rate per grain of Dwek and Werner (1981, their eq. A13), which depends on the grain radius $a$ and the gas temperature through the parameter $\chi=2.71\times10^{8}\,a_{\mu{\rm m}}^{2/3}/T$ and has three regimes. The rate per grain and per unit electron density $n_{\rm e}$ is

H(a,T)=\begin{cases}5.38\times10^{-18}\,a^{2}\,T^{3/2}&\chi\ge4.5\quad\text{(large grains or cold electrons)}\\[2pt]3.37\times10^{-13}\,a^{2.41}\,T^{0.88}&1.5\le\chi<4.5\\[2pt]6.48\times10^{-6}\,a^{3}&\chi<1.5\quad\text{(small grains or hot electrons)}\end{cases}

in cgs units with $a$ in micron. The volumetric rate of a population is $n_{\rm e}\int H(a,T)\,(\mathrm dn/\mathrm da)\,\mathrm da$. The module assumes hydrogen and helium fully ionized, so that $n_{\rm e}=n_{\rm H}\,(2X+Y)/(2X)$ with $X$ and $Y$ the hydrogen and helium mass fractions. The dust cooling is added to the gas cooling rate $\mathrm de/\mathrm dt$ that the tabular cooling integrates; it is always cooling, never heating. Since $\chi\propto a^{2/3}/T$, the two boundaries $\chi=1.5$ and $4.5$ are grain sizes at a given temperature, and a bin can straddle them.

Dust in the time step

The dust update and the gas cooling have to be consistent in time: the temperature of a cell can change by orders of magnitude within one step, and $\dot a$ depends on it steeply, as the figure above showed. The module therefore ties the update to the cooling integration, in one of two modes.

  • subcycle = true (default). The tabular cooling integrates $\mathrm de/\mathrm dt$ in adaptive subcycles, with a Runge–Kutta pair (rk12 or rk45) that sets each subcycle length from its error estimate. After every accepted subcycle the grain bins of the cell are updated over that subcycle length. With time_integrator = euler the rate $\dot a$ is evaluated at the temperature at the start of the subcycle; with heun it is the mean of the rates at the start and end temperatures. The dust cooling is part of $\mathrm de/\mathrm dt$ and so changes with the grain population as it evolves.
  • subcycle = false. The dust is updated once per step, as a first-order split source after all the stages of the integrator, with $\dot a$ at the temperature at the start of the step. The dust cooling then acts only through the tabular cooling, using the grains as they were.

Which one to choose depends on how fast $T$ moves. The first mode follows the temperature through a cooling runaway; the second is cheaper but cannot. Two constraints follow from the design. The townsend cooling integrator is not supported with dust: it needs a single cooling function shared by all cells, whereas the dust cooling of each cell depends on its own grain population. A per-cell variant is sketched on the Townsend integrator page. And the cooling-time timestep limit, when it is on, includes the dust cooling.

The split update sees only the starting temperature, so a cell that starts hot and falls through $T_0$ in the middle of the step is treated as if it stayed hot all along. Subcycling follows the fall, and the Heun variant also removes the first-order error within each subcycle.

Dust injected by AGB winds (next chapter) is added half before and half after each grain-size update, so the same split holds whichever mode runs. In the first mode the cooling is an unsplit source, so the step that the grain update receives in each Runge–Kutta stage is the stage length $\beta_s\Delta t$, the same step the gas cooling receives, as the diagram of the time step above shows.

Where the dust comes from

Dust enters the box in two ways: as an initial population, and, optionally, continuously from the winds of evolved stars. Neither is a model of dust formation in detail; both are meant to give the grain population a sensible starting point and a source.

The initial dust

The initial dust mass of each cell is the gas mass times a dust-to-gas ratio, set by init_profile. The mass is split between the compositions with carbonaceous_grain_mass_fraction and silicate_grain_mass_fraction, and then spread over the bins by init_grainsize_distribution: MRN ($\mathrm dn/\mathrm da\propto a^{-3.5}$; Mathis, Rumpl and Nordsieck 1977), MRN_inverse ($a^{4.5}$, a test distribution), flat, or flat_in_range between two sizes. The bin contents are integrals of the chosen function, so they are exact for the power laws.

  • const_dtg: one dust-to-gas ratio everywhere.
  • vogelsberger_19: a radial profile for clusters, from the fiducial model of Vogelsberger et al. (2019), a cubic in $x=\log_{10}(r/r_{200})$, $\log_{10}{\rm DTG}=0.5x^3+3.58x^2+5.77x-4.06$, capped at $10^{-4}$. The radius $r_{200}$ follows from m_nfw_200 and a critical density of $127\ M_\odot\,{\rm kpc^{-3}}$.
  • stellar_profile: the dust that the AGB winds of the next section would inject over init_run_stellar_injection_time, but given the init_grainsize_distribution rather than the AGB one, to mimic dust that has already been processed.

A floor, dtgfloor, keeps the dust-to-gas ratio from dropping below a chosen value: within the clipping radius, cells with less dust are rescaled up to it, keeping their size distribution, and cells with no dust at all are left empty. With the two non-constant profiles the floor is also applied at initialisation.

A note on the cubic: it is a fit, and it is only a fit over the radii it was made for. Inside about $0.003\,r_{200}$ it climbs back toward the cap, so the central ratio of $10^{-4}$ in this profile comes from the cap and not from the physics of the fit.

AGB winds

With AGB_winds = true the stars of the central galaxy return dust continuously. Three ingredients are combined. The stellar density is a power law, $\rho_\star\propto r^{\gamma_\star}$ (default $\gamma_\star=-2.2$, Cappellari et al. 2015), or a Prugniel–Simien profile, normalised so that the mass between R_lower_in_kpc and R_upper_in_kpc is Mstar_cent_in_Msun, and nothing is injected beyond AGB_max_radius_in_kpc. The dust return per unit stellar mass and time comes from the AGB yield table of Dell'Agli et al. (2017) and a Salpeter initial mass function on $0.3$ to $100\,M_\odot$, with main-sequence lifetimes $t_{\rm MS}=10^4\,{\rm Myr}\,(M/M_\odot)^{-2.5}$:

\dot m_{\rm dust}=\int_{1.5\,M_\odot}^{8\,M_\odot}\frac{\mathrm{IMF}(M)\;m_{\rm dust}(M)}{t_{\rm MS}(M)}\,\mathrm dM\qquad\text{per unit stellar mass}

where $m_{\rm dust}(M)$ is the dust yield of one star of initial mass $M$, interpolated linearly in the table, summed over the columns chosen for each composition. The grain-size distribution of the injected dust is the log-normal-like form of McKinnon et al.,

\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm da}\propto a^{-5}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\ln^2(a/a_{\rm AGB})}{2\sigma_{\rm AGB}^2}\right],\qquad a_{\rm AGB}=0.1\ \mu{\rm m}

whose integrals over each bin, for mass and for number, are computed once at start-up. In each cell the injected mass over a step is the return rate times the cell's stellar mass times the step length; it is added to the bin scalars in the fractions of the distribution, half before and half after the grain-size update.

Tests, parameters and limits

What the repository tests, how the module is switched on, and what it deliberately leaves out.

The one-zone test

The tests in tst/dust_tests/ compare the module with a Monte Carlo model of one uniform box. The box is periodic and filled with gas of $n_{\rm H}=3.0\times10^{-4}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ at $10^7$ K and a dust-to-gas ratio of $10^{-2}$, split equally in mass between carbonaceous and silicate grains with an MRN distribution between $0.05$ and $0.5\ \mu$m. The gas cooling is switched off (disable_all_gas_cooling_for_testing), so the gas cools only through the dust, while sputtering and accretion change the grains. The Monte Carlo code follows individual grains in the same gas with the same grain physics: the same rates, the same Dwek and Werner heating, the same $n_{\rm H}/n_{\rm e}$. The comparison therefore does not test the physics, which is shared by construction. It tests two things that are the numerical content of the module: the discretisation of the size distribution into bins and the time integration.

The convergence study runs the box with 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 bins, with the log-linear reconstruction, the integrated Dwek and Werner cooling, an MRN start, and both compositions. Everything in this section is output of those inputs, run for this page. The repository ships the inputs and the scripts but no results, and the builds in the folder have HDF5 output switched off, so I made a private build with it on and ran the five inputs for 60 Myr. Three changes were needed: the cooling table path, a $16^3$ instead of a $64^3$ mesh (every cell of a uniform box is the same), and a Python Monte Carlo of my own, because the original script needs yt. The reference differs from the repository script in ways that make it cleaner: it uses the code's constants, and instead of random sizes it draws them at regular quantiles of the initial distribution, so it has no sampling noise. Its sensitivity to the step and to the number of grains is about $10^{-3}$ per bin.

One more thing to know before reading the figures. The input template sets subcycle = false, and the generator does not change it, so these runs use the split grain update, once per hydrodynamical step of about 2 Myr with $\dot a$ at the start of the step. The heun setting has no effect there (the code prints a warning saying so), and max_dM_in_bin, which the inputs also set, is not read by this version of the code. The tests therefore check the split scheme.

The first figure compares the bin contents of the grid with the Monte Carlo grains binned the same way. Sputtering dominates in this gas, so every grain shrinks, the distribution moves down in size and the total mass falls. Slide through the 60 Myr and change the number of bins.

Bin contents, AthenaPK against the Monte Carlo from the test runs

Left: the content of each bin as a fraction of the initial total, for the grid (colour) and the Monte Carlo (white lines), with the initial MRN distribution in grey. Right: the difference of the two in each bin. Both compositions are added: they have the same shape, since the rates do not depend on the composition.

The bulk of the distribution agrees at the level of a few $10^{-3}$ or better, and the differences grow smoothly with time. The exception is the top: the two bins below $a_{\max}$ are off by several per cent at the end, one high and one low. This is the one place where the scheme departs visibly from the exact result, and it is understood. The initial distribution has a hard upper edge at $0.5\ \mu$m, and sputtering carries that edge down into the top bins as a sharp step in $\mathrm dn/\mathrm da$. Every step re-reconstructs the bin as a smooth power law, which smears the step a little. A single large shift of the same total size is exact for the same distribution; applied as thirty small shifts, the stand-alone scheme gives $+6.5\%$ and $-8.1\%$ in the two top bins for 16 bins, which are the numbers the AthenaPK run gives ($+6.4\%$ and $-8.1\%$). The smearing is set within the first few steps and then stops growing: the summed error of the bin masses is 1.3% after 3 steps, 1.6% after 30 and 1.7% after 1000, so shorter time steps do not make it worse. It is numerical diffusion at a sharp edge.

The picture is not the textbook one. The error of the grain numbers falls with the number of bins, from $3.6\times10^{-3}$ at 4 bins to $6.6\times10^{-4}$ at 20. The error of the bin masses does not: it goes from 0.6% at 4 bins to 1.6% at 16, and the reason is the one above. The bins that carry the error are the top two, which hold a fifth of the mass at 16 bins, and the rest of the distribution contributes only 0.1% of the total. Narrower bins resolve the edge better, but the error per bin at the edge grows as the bins narrow, so the sum over bins stays about the same. The conclusion for use is practical: with a smooth distribution the bins do converge, and a hard edge in the size distribution is where the scheme should be trusted least.

A note on the repository's reference script

The Monte Carlo script onezone_funcs.py uses a few constants that are not those of the code: the regime coefficient of the Dwek and Werner parameter is $1.71\times10^{8}$ instead of the code's $2.71\times10^{8}$, a megayear is $3.1536\times10^{13}$ s instead of $3.15576\times10^{13}$ s, and the hydrogen mass is $1.673\times10^{-24}$ g instead of $1.6737\times10^{-24}$ g. With the coefficient of the code the three branches of $H(a,T)$ join to within a few per cent, and with the coefficient of the script they jump by about a third at the boundaries, so the script's value looks like a typo. Run with the script's constants, my Monte Carlo moves the temperature at 60 Myr from $-0.18\%$ to $-0.49\%$ of the grid value and leaves the bin masses unchanged to $10^{-4}$. The effect is small, but it is the same size as the offsets one would be trying to explain, and it is worth aligning the script with the code.

The dust is set up through the cluster problem generator: the initial profiles, the dust-to-gas floor and the history files (masses and cooling rates in radius and temperature bins, appended every step) are parameters of <problem/cluster/...>. Since the history output is on by default, a run with dust must either set its radial and temperature ranges or switch it off with write_to_file = false.

What it leaves out

Not in the module yet: shattering and coagulation, temperature-dependent sticking efficiencies, supernova dust, a more complete star-formation and AGB injection prescription, and a metal model in which accretion depletes the gas phase and sputtering enriches it. These are the items listed as open in the repository documentation.
  • The dust is passive. There is no drag, no decoupling from the gas and no back-reaction other than the cooling. Grains and gas are at the same place and move together, which is a good approximation for small grains in hot, tenuous gas and a poor one wherever the grains decouple.
  • The metals are a fixed reservoir. Accretion uses a constant metallicity of $0.33\,{\rm Z}_\odot$ and never exhausts it; sputtering does not return metals to the gas.
  • One size-independent $\dot a$. The bookkeeping depends on it. Processes whose rate depends on the grain size, such as shattering or coagulation, would need a different scheme for the bins.
  • One cooling integrator at a time. The Townsend integrator is not supported with dust, as explained above.
  • The cluster problem generator only. The initial conditions, the floor and the history output are implemented there.
