Submillimeter instruments

ALMA in the Chilean Andes and NOEMA in the French Alps are not single telescopes but arrays of dishes that work together as one instrument, an interferometer. Each pair of dishes measures one ingredient of the image, and the image is built afterwards by computer. This page explains how, from the reason why millimeter astronomy needs arrays to the way the signals are captured, calibrated and turned into the images and spectral cubes that appear in papers.

It follows one thread: a pair of antennas measures one Fourier component of the sky. Chapter 2 shows what a pair measures, Chapter 3 how many pairs and the rotation of the Earth fill in the picture, Chapter 4 how an image is recovered, Chapter 5 the hardware and the atmosphere, Chapter 6 the calibration that makes the data usable, and Chapter 7 compares the two observatories and shows what the data look like. Every chapter and section opens with a short box saying where it is going, and a ✦ marks a section with an interactive figure. The models are deliberately simple and every figure says what it assumes; numbers about the observatories are from their public documentation, and a few are from memory and flagged as such.

Why millimeter telescopes are arrays

In this chapterWhy a single dish cannot give a sharp image at millimeter wavelengths, and how a set of dishes gets around it by acting as pieces of one enormous telescope.

Millimeter waves tell us about cold gas and dust: the molecular clouds where stars form, the disks where planets form, and the gas of distant galaxies. They are also 2000 times longer than visible light, and that one fact decides how the telescopes have to be built.

Diffraction and the size of a dish

In this sectionThe sharpness of a telescope is set by its diameter in units of the wavelength. At 1.3 mm a dish would have to be about 16 km across to match ALMA.

A telescope cannot focus a point source to a point: the waves are diffracted by the edge of the aperture, and the image of a star is a small disk with rings. The width of the central disk is

\theta \;\approx\; 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}

where $\theta$ is the angular resolution in radians, $\lambda$ the wavelength and $D$ the diameter of the aperture (Rayleigh's criterion for a circular aperture). What matters is the ratio $\lambda/D$. The Hubble Space Telescope ($D=2.4$ m) looks at 550 nm and gets 0.06″. Your eye (7 mm pupil) gets about 20″. A radio dish looking at 1.3 mm is observing at a wavelength 2400 times longer, so to have the sharpness of Hubble it would need to be 2400 times larger, about 6 km across.

The punchline. One 12-m dish at 1.3 mm sees details of about 27″, the size of a small nebula. ALMA sees 0.02″, a thousand times finer, which a single dish could only match if it were 16 km across. Nobody can build such a dish, and nobody has to.

Many small dishes, one big telescope

In this sectionThe idea of aperture synthesis: a few small pieces of a huge mirror are enough to measure the details, as long as they are placed far apart. Which property of the array controls which property of the image.

Imagine a mirror 16 km wide covered by a mask with only two small holes. It would collect very little light, but each pair of holes, separated by $B$, would still produce interference fringes that carry information about details of size $\lambda/B$. An interferometer is this: a set of dishes spread over the ground, each one a small hole in a giant virtual mirror. The distance between two dishes projected on the sky is called their baseline. $N$ dishes give $N(N-1)/2$ baselines (1225 for ALMA's 50 dishes, 66 for NOEMA's 12), and each baseline records one number per second and per frequency channel, a visibility, that describes one angular scale and one orientation of the sky. The Earth's rotation then makes the baselines sweep through new orientations, so that after several hours a sparse array has sampled a large part of the virtual mirror. This is aperture synthesis (Ryle, who developed it, shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics).

Each property of the image is set by a different property of the array, which is why it is useful to keep four numbers in mind:

Sharpness
Set by the longest baseline $B_{\max}$: the finest detail is about $\lambda/B_{\max}$. This is why ALMA's most extended configuration reaches 20 milliarcseconds.
Sensitivity
Set by the total collecting area, $N\pi D^2/4$, the integration time and the noise of the receivers and sky (Chapter 5). More dishes means fainter sources, but not finer detail.
Largest structure
Set by the shortest baseline $B_{\min}$: structure much larger than $\lambda/B_{\min}$ is invisible (Chapter 4). Dishes cannot be closer than their own diameter.
Field of view
Set by the size of one dish: a single pointing covers only about $1.13\lambda/D$ (the primary beam), about 25″ for a 12-m dish at 1.3 mm. Larger regions are mapped with several pointings (a mosaic).

The two observatories are two different compromises. ALMA (Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, a partnership of ESO, the US National Science Foundation with NRAO, and Japan's NINS with NAOJ, in cooperation with Chile) stands on the Chajnantor plateau in the Atacama desert at 5000 m. It has 50 dishes of 12 m (the main array), a compact array of twelve 7-m dishes (the ACA, or Morita array) and four more 12-m dishes used as single telescopes ("total power"); the main array can be spread over up to 16 km. NOEMA (Northern Extended Millimeter Array, operated by IRAM, funded by CNRS in France, the Max Planck Society in Germany and the IGN in Spain) stands on the Plateau de Bure in the French Alps at 2550 m, with twelve 15-m dishes running on rails over about 1.7 km. Chapter 7 puts the two side by side.

The punchline. An interferometer trades collecting area for sharpness: it throws away most of the virtual mirror and keeps only the pieces that tell you about small scales. The price is that the image has to be reconstructed by computer, which is the subject of the next three chapters.

Two antennas and a fringe

In this chapterWhat a single pair of antennas measures: a fringe pattern whose spacing is $\lambda/B$, and a visibility whose amplitude says how much of the source is smaller than that.

Everything about interferometry follows from what two antennas can measure. This chapter builds it up in two steps: first for a point source, where only the position matters, then for an extended source, where the size matters too.

Two dishes and a delay

In this sectionA wave from a source off the axis reaches the two dishes at different times; multiplying the two signals gives a fringe whose phase says where the source is.

Two dishes a distance $B$ apart observe a distant source in a direction at an angle $\theta$ from the direction perpendicular to the baseline. The wave is flat (the source is very far away), so it reaches one dish before the other, by an extra path $B\sin\theta\approx B\theta$ and a geometric delay

\tau_g=\frac{B\sin\theta}{c}\approx\frac{B\theta}{c}

The correlator multiplies the two signals after cancelling this delay for the pointing center, and averages. For a point source at an angle $\theta$ from the center the residual delay leaves a phase difference $2\pi\nu\tau_g$, and the averaged product is

r(\theta)\;\propto\;A(\theta)\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi B\theta}{\lambda}\Big)

where $\nu$ is the frequency, $\lambda=c/\nu$ the wavelength, and $A(\theta)$ the response of a single dish, its primary beam, about $1.13\lambda/D$ wide. This is Young's double-slit experiment with antennas for slits. The pattern repeats every $\lambda/B$ in angle, the fringe spacing. Move the slider and watch the sample marked on the curve: a displacement of the source by a fraction of the fringe spacing changes the output a lot, so the pair is extremely sensitive to the position of the source at the scale $\lambda/B$, and completely insensitive to larger or smaller details.

The visibility of an extended source

In this sectionA real source is a sum of point sources, and its fringes partly cancel: the stronger the cancellation, the more the source is resolved by that baseline. The result is the Fourier transform of the sky.

A real source is a collection of points, and each one produces its own fringe pattern with a different phase. If the source is much smaller than the fringe spacing, all the fringes add up in step and the pair sees a strong signal. If the source is as large as a fringe or larger, bright and dark parts of the fringes overlap and cancel. The amplitude of the combined fringe, normalized to the total flux, is the visibility amplitude $|V|$, between 1 (a point) and 0 (completely resolved). Together with the phase it forms a complex number, the visibility, which is a single Fourier component of the sky brightness $I(l,m)$ (the van Cittert-Zernike theorem):

V(u,v)=\iint A(l,m)\,I(l,m)\,e^{-2\pi i\,(ul+vm)}\,dl\,dm

Here $(l,m)$ are the two angular coordinates on the sky (in radians, measured from the pointing center), $A$ the primary beam, and $(u,v)=(B_x,B_y)/\lambda$ the baseline projected perpendicular to the line of sight and measured in wavelengths. A baseline of length $B$ is therefore sensitive to the spatial frequency $u=B/\lambda$, in cycles per radian, which corresponds to details of size $1/u=\lambda/B$.

Three things to try. Choose the Gaussian and lengthen the baseline: the amplitude falls from 1 to 0 as the baseline goes from much shorter than $\lambda/\theta$ to much longer, so a long baseline cannot see a large source (it is resolved out). Choose the pair of points: the amplitude oscillates and has nulls where the fringe of the baseline puts one source on a bright and the other on a dark stripe, so the visibility versus baseline length measures the separation, as for a binary star. Choose the point: the amplitude is 1 at every baseline.

The punchline. Short baselines see big things, long baselines see small things, and each baseline only answers one question: "how much of the sky has structure at my scale and orientation?" Collecting the answers for all baselines gives the Fourier transform of the sky, and the next chapter explains why that is not yet enough to make an image.

Filling the uv plane

In this chapterHow the antennas of an array, and the rotation of the Earth, sample the Fourier transform of the sky, and what the pattern of samples tells you about the image you will get.

Each baseline measures one point of the Fourier transform of the sky, so the quality of an image depends on how many points are measured and where they are. The plane of those points, the uv plane, is the most useful picture of an interferometer.

The array and its uv coverage

In this sectionAn interactive tour of the uv plane: choose an array, a source declination and an observing time, and see which spatial frequencies are measured.

The uv plane is the plane of spatial frequencies: the point $(u,v)$ is the baseline vector, seen from the source and measured in wavelengths. A baseline between two antennas whose ground positions differ by (east, north) $=(b_E,b_N)$ appears, from a source at declination $\delta$ and hour angle $H$ (the angle the source has moved from the meridian), at

u=\frac{L_x\sin H+L_y\cos H}{\lambda},\qquad v=\frac{-L_x\sin\delta\cos H+L_y\sin\delta\sin H+L_z\cos\delta}{\lambda}

with $(L_x,L_y,L_z)=(-b_N\sin\varphi,\ b_E,\ b_N\cos\varphi)$ the baseline in equatorial coordinates (flat ground) and $\varphi$ the latitude of the site (Thompson, Moran and Swenson 2017). What matters is that the baseline projected on the sky changes with time, as the Earth turns: each baseline traces an ellipse in the uv plane, and the baseline $-\mathbf{B}$ (the same pair seen the other way) traces the mirror ellipse. A source that is high in the sky sees the full ground baseline; a source near the horizon sees it foreshortened.

The punchline. A snapshot samples only a sparse sprinkle of points; Earth rotation turns each point into an arc and fills the plane in. Resolution is set by the outer edge of the sampled region and image quality by how completely it is filled.

What the shape of the coverage tells you

In this sectionHow the layout of the array, the declination of the source and the length of the observation decide the shape of the beam, and why arrays are moved between configurations.

Several things can be read directly from the figure above:

  • An east-west line of antennas gives elliptical tracks and an elongated beam. NOEMA, with a long east-west rail and a short north-south one, fills the plane with flattened ellipses that, for a source near the celestial equator, leave little coverage in $v$, so the beam is elongated north-south; at a high declination the tracks become circles and the beam is round. The beam is also elongated for sources at low elevation, as the projected baselines shrink in the north-south direction.
  • A two-dimensional layout gives rounder coverage. ALMA's configurations are spirals with arms in three directions, so as to cover the plane evenly even in a short observation and to give a round beam at any declination.
  • Gaps in the coverage produce sidelobes. The Fourier transform of a pattern of samples is the dirty beam (next chapter); holes in the coverage become rings and stripes around every source.
  • The same array can be reconfigured. ALMA's 12-m antennas are moved between concrete pads with giant transporters, over several months, so that the array can be compact (about 160 m: coarse resolution, but sensitive to large structures) or extended (16 km: the finest detail, but blind to large structures); NOEMA's antennas run on rails between stations. The choice of configuration is a choice of the range of scales to observe.
  • More time means more coverage, not more resolution. The outer edge of the sampled region does not change with time, only how well it is filled.
The punchline. The array does not take a picture; it takes a set of Fourier samples, and the only thing that decides which structures in the sky can be recovered is the range of spacings that were sampled. The next chapter shows what you get out of a given set.

From visibilities to an image

In this chapterHow the measured visibilities are turned into an image: the Fourier transform gives a "dirty" image, deconvolution (CLEAN) cleans it, and structures that are too large for the shortest baselines are lost.

If the whole uv plane were measured, the image would be the inverse Fourier transform of the visibilities. It is not: the array samples only some points. This chapter shows what that does to an image and how astronomers undo part of it.

The dirty image and the dirty beam

In this sectionTaking the inverse Fourier transform of the measured points gives the true sky convolved with a pattern of sidelobes, the dirty beam, which is itself the transform of the uv coverage.

Let $S(u,v)$ be the sampling function: 1 where a baseline measured a visibility and 0 elsewhere (in practice a weight). The inverse Fourier transform of the sampled visibilities is the dirty image

I_D(l,m)=\iint S(u,v)\,V(u,v)\,e^{2\pi i(ul+vm)}\,du\,dv\;=\;I(l,m)\ast B_D(l,m),\qquad B_D=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[S]

where $\ast$ is a convolution and $B_D$ is the dirty beam, the image of a point source: the transform of the uv coverage itself. It has a narrow central peak, of width about $\lambda/B_{\max}$, which sets the resolution, and sidelobes that come from the holes in the coverage. Every source in the sky is replaced by a copy of the dirty beam, scaled by its flux, so a faint source near a bright one is buried in the sidelobes of the bright one. The information that falls in a hole in the uv plane was never measured, and no computation can bring it back without assumptions.

The punchline. What the telescope delivers is the sky convolved with the beam of the array, not the sky. The job of imaging is to deconvolve it, that is to guess the most plausible sky that is compatible with the measured Fourier points.

Deconvolution: CLEAN

In this sectionHow the CLEAN algorithm removes the sidelobes by assuming the sky is a collection of points, and what it cannot do.

The most used algorithm is CLEAN (Högbom 1974), which assumes the sky is made of point sources:

  1. Find the brightest pixel of the dirty image (the residual image at the start).
  2. Record a point-source "component" of a small fraction of its flux, the loop gain (here 0.1).
  3. Subtract the dirty beam, scaled and centered on that pixel, from the residual image. This removes the source and its sidelobes.
  4. Repeat until the brightest residual is comparable to the noise.
  5. Convolve the list of components with a clean beam, a Gaussian fitted to the central peak of the dirty beam (blue ellipse in the figure), and add the residual image back so that the noise is still visible. The result is the CLEAN image whose units are flux per beam.

The restored image has no sidelobes, and an angular resolution set by the clean beam; there is no gain in real resolution beyond it. Several refinements exist: multi-scale CLEAN (components are Gaussians of several sizes, much better for extended emission), clean masks (components are allowed only where there is emission), and weighting. In natural weighting every visibility counts equally, which gives the lowest noise and a broad beam, dominated by the many short baselines; in uniform weighting each uv cell counts equally, which gives the narrowest beam but more noise; Briggs weighting, controlled by a "robust" parameter, sits between the two. The figure uses natural weighting.

The punchline. CLEAN does not add information; it uses the assumption that the sky is sparse to extrapolate over the uv holes. It works well for compact sources and less well for smooth extended emission, where the assumption fails.

Missing short spacings

In this sectionWhy a large cloud is invisible to an array whose shortest baseline is long, and how the ACA and single dishes fill the gap.

An array cannot have baselines shorter than the diameter of its dishes (they would collide), and in practice the shortest are 15 m for ALMA's 12-m dishes and 24 m for NOEMA's 15-m dishes (IRAM; ALMA Technical Handbook). The shortest baseline $B_{\min}$ fixes the largest scale the array measures at all,

\theta_{\max}\;\sim\;\frac{\lambda}{B_{\min}}

(the ALMA Technical Handbook quotes about $0.98\,\lambda/B_{\min}$ for the "maximum recoverable scale", and it is a smaller scale for a thin uv coverage). At 1.3 mm and 15 m this is 18″, and in an extended configuration where the shortest baselines are hundreds of meters, a few arcseconds. Emission smoother than that has its Fourier components in the hole at the center of the uv plane and is resolved out: it does not appear in the image, and the dirty image has a "negative bowl" around bright sources (the blue halo in the figure above when the short spacings are off). In the figure, for the cloud the readout "fraction of the sky's Fourier power measured" rises from about 75% to 100% when the short spacings are added, and the blue bowl around the cloud nearly disappears.

The ACA (7-m array)
Twelve 7-m dishes packed in a compact array, at baselines from about 9 m to 45 m, measure the scales that the main array misses, up to a few tens of arcseconds at 230 GHz, and their data are combined with those of the 12-m array.
Total power
A single dish measures all the Fourier components up to its size, including the zero spacing (the total flux). ALMA uses 12-m total-power antennas; NOEMA data are combined with maps of the IRAM 30-m telescope.
Combination
The single-dish map is combined with the interferometer image, either in the visibility plane (joint deconvolution) or in the image plane by adding their Fourier transforms with weights that follow the beams ("feathering").
Mosaicking
Fields larger than a primary beam are covered with a grid of overlapping pointings, which are imaged jointly; a mosaic recovers somewhat larger scales than one pointing, because baselines shorter than the dish diameter are in effect synthesized.
The punchline. An interferometer sees only the structure whose scale lies between $\lambda/B_{\max}$ and $\lambda/B_{\min}$. This is why a high-resolution image of a molecular cloud can show the dense cores and nothing of the cloud, and why a flux measured with an array can be much smaller than the flux of a single dish.

From the sky to the correlator

In this chapterHow the signal travels from the sky to the visibilities: the atmosphere and the choice of site, the receivers, the frequency conversion, and the correlator that multiplies every pair.

So far the antennas were ideal. This chapter follows a photon of 230 GHz through the real hardware, and explains why the observatories are where they are.

The atmosphere and the choice of site

In this sectionWater vapor absorbs millimeter waves, so the arrays are at high, dry sites. An interactive plot of the atmospheric transmission, the receiver bands of both observatories, and where a spectral line lands for a source at a given redshift.

Air is almost transparent at centimeter wavelengths and opaque in much of the millimeter and submillimeter range, because of the rotational lines of water vapor (at 22, 183, 325, 380, 448, 557, 752 GHz and higher) and oxygen (the complex near 60 GHz and a line at 118.75 GHz), and the continuum from the wings of all of them. The amount of water that matters is the column along the line of sight, quoted as the precipitable water vapor (PWV): the depth of liquid water that would result if all the vapor above the antenna condensed. Water vapor is concentrated near the ground (its scale height is about 2 km), so the cure is altitude and dryness. ALMA's plateau at 5000 m in the Atacama, one of the driest places on Earth, has a median PWV of about 1 mm; the Plateau de Bure at 2550 m has typically a few millimeters, and so is better at the lower-frequency windows (the 3 mm, 2 mm and 1.3 mm bands) and cannot observe at the highest frequencies.

The figure is a schematic model: a sum of the main lines and a continuum calibrated on typical values for dry sites, not a radiative-transfer calculation (ALMA uses the "ATM" model for that). The receiver bands are the frequency ranges covered by the receivers. The lines are those that matter for the cold gas of the molecular gas page: the CO ladder, the carbon lines and the fine-structure lines of ionized gas. Move the redshift and watch which receiver band each line falls in, and in what part of the absorption lines.

Three lessons. First, there are windows (the receiver bands sit in them) separated by absorption lines: a line that falls on an absorption feature is lost, even if it is in a band. Second, transmission degrades quickly with frequency and water: at 650 GHz an increase from 0.5 to 1 mm is a large loss, so the submillimeter bands are used only in the driest weather, which is also why the schedule matches the weather to the observation. Third, the atmosphere is not only an absorber: it also emits (it is warm), and it fluctuates, which is the source of the noise and phase errors of Chapter 6.

The punchline. Where a line can be observed depends on its redshift, the receiver bands and the weather, in that order. The arrays are built on the highest dry places on Earth because there is nothing else to do about the water.

Heterodyne reception: from 230 GHz to a few GHz

In this sectionWhy the receivers shift the frequency down before doing anything else, which sideband is received, and how a spectral line is placed in the band.

Amplifying and sampling a signal at hundreds of GHz is hard; doing it at a few GHz is routine. The receivers therefore start by mixing the sky signal, at frequency $\nu_{\rm sky}$, with a very pure sine wave of frequency $\nu_{\rm LO}$, the local oscillator (LO). The product of two sinusoids contains the sum and the difference of the frequencies,

\cos(2\pi\nu_{\rm sky}t)\cos(2\pi\nu_{\rm LO}t)=\tfrac12\cos\!\big[2\pi(\nu_{\rm sky}-\nu_{\rm LO})t\big]+\tfrac12\cos\!\big[2\pi(\nu_{\rm sky}+\nu_{\rm LO})t\big]

and a filter keeps the difference, the intermediate frequency $\nu_{\rm IF}=|\nu_{\rm sky}-\nu_{\rm LO}|$. This works as a copy of the sky spectrum shifted down by $\nu_{\rm LO}$, with its phases and its amplitudes preserved, which is all the interferometer needs. The same $\nu_{\rm IF}$ comes from two sky frequencies, one above and one below the LO, the upper and lower sideband. A receiver that cannot tell them apart is "double sideband" (DSB), and the noise of both bands adds up; a "sideband-separating" (2SB) receiver outputs the two separately. The LO is tuned so that the line of interest falls inside the IF band in the wanted sideband.

The IF band drawn is 4 to 12 GHz, as at NOEMA (two sidebands of 8 GHz each, per polarization); ALMA's bands use 4 GHz per sideband in most cases and different values in some. The line is CO(2-1), rest frequency 230.538 GHz. The velocity width converts to a frequency width as $\Delta\nu/\nu=\Delta v/c$.

The punchline. The tuning is a choice of LO frequency: it decides which part of the sky spectrum, in two bands on each side of the LO, lands in the IF band that the correlator sees. A source at a redshift that moves the line out of both is not observed, however good the receiver.

The correlator: multiplying every pair

In this sectionWhy multiplying and averaging the signals of two antennas recovers the source even when each antenna's signal is almost all noise, and how large the computation is.

The signal of the source in one antenna is tiny compared to the noise of the receiver and sky (the source is typically 0.1% or less of the total power). The noise of different antennas is independent but the signal from the source is common, so the product of the two voltages averaged over many samples tends to the power of the source and the noise averages away. The statistical uncertainty falls as $1/\sqrt{M}$ with the number of independent samples $M=2\Delta\nu\,t$ (bandwidth $\Delta\nu$ and integration time $t$, as for any radiometer), which is why bandwidth is so valuable.

Averaging the product of two noisy signals animation

Each sample is $x_i=s+n_i$ at the two antennas, with $s$ the common source signal and $n_i$ independent unit-variance noise. The plot is the running mean of $x_1x_2$ divided by the true power of $s$. The expected scatter is $\sqrt{(1+r)^2+r^2}/(r\sqrt{M})$ for $r$ the source-to-noise power ratio and $M$ samples (jointly Gaussian signals).

The correlator does this for every pair, every frequency channel and every second or so. It also does something else first: because the signal reaches the antennas at different times, it must delay the signal of the nearer antennas by exactly the geometric delay $\tau_g$ of the pointing center, which changes continuously as the Earth rotates (up to $B/c=53\ \mu$s for 16 km). The delay is applied to the digital samples, with a precision of fractions of a sample. Then the correlator computes the correlation between each pair as a function of time lag and Fourier transforms it into a cross-power spectrum, or does the Fourier transform of each signal first and multiplies (the FX design of NOEMA's PolyFiX). The scale of the task is $N(N-1)/2$ pairs times all the channels: 1225 pairs for 50 antennas, 2016 for 64, and 66 for NOEMA's 12, each for tens of thousands of channels.

The punchline. The interferometer works because the noise is different in each antenna and the sky is the same: a million samples turn a source invisible in each antenna alone into a clear detection of the pair.

Calibration: undoing the atmosphere

In this chapterWhy the raw visibilities cannot be used directly, how fluctuating water vapor corrupts their phase, and how observing a nearby calibrator regularly removes most of the error.

A visibility is only useful if its phase and amplitude are the sky's. Between the sky and the correlator lie the atmosphere and the electronics, which both change them. Calibration measures and removes those changes, and it is what makes observing with an interferometer a sequence of short visits, not a long stare.

Why the phase is the problem

In this sectionWater vapor changes the length of the optical path above each antenna, and the phase error that results grows with baseline and with frequency.

Water vapor slows radio waves, in a way that does not depend on the wavelength across the millimeter band: the extra path length through the atmosphere is about 6 mm for each millimeter of precipitable water vapor above the antenna (the "wet delay"). Water is not uniformly spread but is carried by the wind in blobs, so the column above antenna 1 and that above antenna 2 differ a little, and the difference changes with time. A fluctuation of only 0.1 mm of PWV between two lines of sight is 0.6 mm of extra path, 0.46 of a wavelength (166°) at 1.3 mm. The phase error is

\delta\phi=\frac{2\pi\,\delta L}{\lambda}

where $\delta L$ is the path difference. It is large at short wavelengths, and it grows with the baseline: two antennas close together look through nearly the same atmosphere, two antennas kilometers apart through different blobs. Turbulence theory and measurements at millimeter sites give an rms phase error that rises as a power of the baseline, with an exponent between about 1/3 and 5/6 depending on the baseline and the thickness of the turbulent layer (Carilli and Holdaway 1999); the figure below uses an exponent of 0.6, in between. A phase error does two kinds of damage: if the error changes during an integration, the average of the visibility has a smaller amplitude, by $e^{-\sigma_\phi^2/2}$ for an rms error $\sigma_\phi$ (a decorrelation, which lowers the flux); and an error different on every baseline moves the sources and spreads their flux over the image (the image is blurred, with extra sidelobes).

The model is $\sigma_\phi=\sigma_{1\,{\rm km}}(\nu/230\,{\rm GHz})(B/1\,{\rm km})^{0.6}$, a power law with an intermediate exponent (the range is discussed by Carilli and Holdaway 1999), and not a measurement; real values vary with the weather by large factors and flatten at large baselines. The 183 GHz water vapor radiometers of ALMA correct part of the error; a factor of two is used here for illustration, and the correction is in practice better in some conditions and worse in others.

The punchline. The same weather that is perfect for a compact array at 3 mm is useless for the longest baselines at 0.45 mm. This is why high-resolution, high-frequency observations are scheduled only in the best weather, and why most of the effort in operating these arrays goes into predicting the phase.

Phase referencing: the calibration cycle

In this sectionHow the array alternates between the target and a nearby quasar to track the phase, and the trade-off in choosing the time between calibrator scans.

The cure is to measure the error on a source whose true phase is known, a bright, compact quasar close to the target (a few degrees away, so that the line of sight goes through nearly the same atmosphere). A quasar is unresolved, so its true visibility has zero phase and the measured phase is the error. The array switches between the calibrator and the target at regular intervals, and the errors measured on the calibrator, interpolated in time, are subtracted from the target. This is phase referencing. A typical sequence is: calibrator, target, target, target, calibrator, and so on, and every few cycles a check source to verify that the method works.

The choice of the cycle time is a trade-off. The shorter the cycle, the better the interpolation, but the more time is spent on the calibrator, not the target. A longer cycle gives more time on the target, but the phase wanders further between two calibrator scans. The net signal-to-noise ratio is proportional to the coherence (the fraction of the amplitude kept) times the square root of the fraction of time on target. The punchline. For the same atmosphere there is a best cycle time, and it is short when the phase wanders fast: at the highest frequencies and longest baselines an array can spend more than half of its time on the calibrator.

What an uncorrected phase does to an image

In this sectionThe imaging simulator of Chapter 4 with a random phase error added to each antenna, to see how a residual error blurs and distorts the image.

Take the simulator of Chapter 4 and give each antenna a random phase error at each time sample. The error on a baseline is the difference of the errors of its two antennas. Use the presets to go from perfect calibration, through a good night with the radiometers, to bad weather. In this toy the errors are independent from one sample to the next, so their main effects are a loss of peak brightness (the visibilities add up with slightly different phases) and a growing level of noise-like artifacts. Real atmospheric errors are correlated in time, and then they also shift and distort the sources.

The punchline. Phase errors of 20 to 30° already cost 5% to 15% of the flux, and at 60° or more half of it is lost and the image is no longer trustworthy. This is the budget that sets what ALMA and NOEMA can do on a given day.

The other calibrations

In this sectionBandpass, flux scale, pointing, and self-calibration: the other things that have to be corrected.
Bandpass
The electronics do not respond equally at all frequencies. A bright, featureless quasar observed for a few minutes shows the response of each antenna channel by channel, which is then divided out. Without it spectral lines would be mixed with ripples of the band.
Flux scale
The correlator output is in arbitrary units. A source of known flux density (a planet, a moon such as Titan, or a quasar whose flux is monitored by the observatory) sets the conversion to janskys, at a precision of 5 to 10% in the millimeter band.
Pointing and focus
The primary beam is only a few tens of arcseconds wide, so the dishes must point to a small fraction of it. Quasars are observed at intervals to refine the pointing and focus of each antenna.
Self-calibration
When the target itself is bright and compact enough, its own data can be used as the calibrator: the image is used as a model, the errors on each antenna are solved for and removed, and the process is repeated. It can improve the dynamic range of a strong source by a large factor.
The punchline. A large fraction of an observing session is spent on sources that are not the target. They are what turns an array of dishes into a measuring instrument.

Which scales can each facility see?

In this sectionFor a chosen frequency, the finest and coarsest structures each facility measures, compared with the size of a protoplanetary disk or a distant clump.

Resolution, largest recoverable scale and field of view of each facility depend on the frequency in the same way, as $\lambda$ over a length. At 230 GHz, for example, the most extended ALMA configuration resolves 0.02″, while the whole disk of a nearby young star, about 1.4″ in diameter, is more than ten times the size of the beam, and a one-kiloparsec star-forming clump at redshift 2, 0.12″, needs an intermediate configuration.

Single dishes see every scale larger than their own beam. These are rounded values, and one array only reaches the extreme scales with the matching configuration. Angular size of the clump: 1 kpc at $z=2$ is about 0.12″ ($\sim$8.4 kpc/″ for a standard cosmology).

The punchline. Asking "what resolution can I get?" is not enough. The question is which range of scales the science needs, and the answer is a combination of configurations (and often of the 7-m array and total power) that covers it.

What the data look like: the spectral cube

In this sectionAn ALMA or NOEMA observation of a spectral line is a cube with two sky axes and a velocity axis; a rotating disk is the classic example.

The correlator measures all frequency channels at once, so imaging each channel gives a stack of images, a cube with axes (right ascension, declination, frequency), where frequency is converted into a Doppler velocity $v=c(\nu_0-\nu)/\nu_0$ along the line of sight. A rotating disk, seen inclined by $i$, has gas that moves towards us on one side and away from us on the other, and at each velocity the line comes from a different strip. Summing the cube over velocity gives the moment 0 map, the integrated emission; the intensity-weighted mean velocity gives the moment 1 map, the velocity field; and the sum over the sky gives the spectrum, which for a rotating disk has the "double horn" shape with a width of $2v\sin i$. These maps are how the rotation of galaxies, the mass of black holes and the kinematics of protoplanetary disks are measured.

The punchline. A cube is a three-dimensional measurement, and the spectrum at each pixel is as important as the image. A disk shows up as two velocity "horns" and a gradient across the sky, and a single channel shows only the slice of the disk that moves at that velocity.

From proposal to archive

In this sectionThe path of an observation: how time is allocated, what an observing block contains and what the observer gets back.
  1. Proposal. Observing time is allocated by peer review in yearly cycles (ALMA) or twice-yearly calls (IRAM). The proposal specifies the sources, the frequencies and spectral setup, the resolution and largest scale wanted (which fixes the configuration), and the sensitivity, from the observatory's sensitivity calculator, which implements the radiometer equation of Chapter 5 with the real system temperatures and weather.
  2. Scheduling blocks. Each accepted project is turned into scheduling blocks of 30 to 90 minutes or so, each with its calibrators and a target section. The blocks are executed dynamically, according to the weather, the phase stability and the availability of the configuration.
  3. Execution. The array points to the calibrators (bandpass, flux, phase), tunes the receivers, and starts the cycle of Chapter 6. Operators at the site and in the control building monitor the data quality.
  4. Calibration and imaging. The observatory runs a pipeline to calibrate the visibilities and make a first image or cube (CASA for ALMA); users can redo it by hand. NOEMA data are calibrated with CLIC and imaged with MAPPING in GILDAS.
  5. Archive. Calibrated data and products are delivered to the investigators and after a proprietary period (12 months as a standard for ALMA) become public in the archive.
The punchline. Radio interferometry is a pipeline: the observer's choices at the proposal stage (frequency, configuration, sensitivity) decide the range of scales and the noise of the final cube long before the first photon arrives.

Beyond the images

In this sectionOther ways these arrays are used and what is coming.
Very long baselines
ALMA and NOEMA can be phased: the signals of the dishes are added with the right delays and the whole array acts as a single, very sensitive dish in a network of telescopes across the world. The Event Horizon Telescope uses this to reach baselines of an Earth diameter at 1.3 mm (ALMA Phasing System; Matthews et al. 2018).
Polarization
With two polarizations per antenna the arrays measure the four Stokes parameters, which give the magnetic field direction in clouds (dust polarization) and in jets.
Upgrades
ALMA plans a Wideband Sensitivity Upgrade, with a larger instantaneous bandwidth and a new correlator; NOEMA has been extended in recent years with receivers and rails. Plans and dates are announced by the observatories.
