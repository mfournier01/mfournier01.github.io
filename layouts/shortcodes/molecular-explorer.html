Molecular gas and thermochemistry

Gas in galaxies and clusters spans eight orders of magnitude in temperature, from cold molecular cores at 10 K to the intracluster medium at $10^8$ K. What decides which phase a parcel of gas is in is a competition between heating and cooling, and what decides what the gas is made of is a competition between formation and destruction of molecules. This page follows both: how hydrogen becomes molecular and why it needs shielding, how carbon monoxide traces what hydrogen hides, how chemical networks are built and when they can be simplified, how molecular gas relates to star formation in nearby galaxies, and how the same thermal physics produces cold filaments in the cores of galaxy clusters.

It is written at the level of a graduate course and keeps theory and observations side by side. A ✦ marks a section with an interactive figure. The models behind the figures are deliberately simple (plane-parallel slabs, fitting formulas, one-zone balances), and every figure says what it assumes.

Multiphase gas, from the ISM to the ICM

Gas does not come in a continuous range of temperatures. It piles up in a few distinct phases, because heating and cooling balance only at special temperatures. The same logic holds from the 10 K cores of molecular clouds to the $10^8$ K gas between galaxies, and it is the thread of this page.

The phases of the gas

The classical picture of the interstellar medium has a handful of phases that coexist in rough pressure equilibrium (Field, Goldsmith and Habing 1969; McKee and Ostriker 1977). Writing the thermal pressure as $P/k_B=nT$, the Milky Way disk sits near $P/k_B\sim3\times10^3\ {\rm K\,cm^{-3}}$ (Jenkins and Tripp 2011), and a phase is a region of the density-temperature plane along that isobar. Molecular clouds and the cores of clusters of galaxies have far higher pressures, but the same structure appears: a hot, tenuous phase and a cold, dense one sharing a pressure.

Two things stand out. The ISM phases do not follow a single curve: a warm and a cold neutral phase (WNM and CNM) share a pressure but differ by a factor of about 100 in temperature and in density, which is the signature of the thermal instability of Chapter 2. And the cold gas found in cluster cores, Hα filaments at $10^4$ K and molecular filaments below 100 K, sits at the pressure of the hot cluster gas around it, $P/k_B\sim10^6$ to $10^7\ {\rm K\,cm^{-3}}$, a thousand times above the pressure of the Galactic ISM. The same physics, at a different pressure.

How each phase is observed

No single observation sees all the gas. Each tracer is excited, or absorbs, over a limited range of temperatures and densities, and so it is blind to everything else. Cold gas is seen through line emission of atoms and molecules and through dust; warm ionized gas through recombination lines; and hot gas through thermal X-rays and the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect.

Heating, cooling and thermal instability

A gas settles where the energy it radiates equals the energy it absorbs. The cooling function sets how steeply the loss rate depends on temperature, and whether the equilibrium that results is stable decides if gas stays at that temperature or splits into two phases.

The cooling function

For optically thin gas of hydrogen density $n_{\rm H}$, the energy lost per unit volume and time is written $n_{\rm H}^2\Lambda(T)$, with the cooling function $\Lambda(T)$ in ${\rm erg\,cm^3\,s^{-1}}$. It sums every channel by which collisions put gas into an excited state that then radiates. The timescale for the gas to cool at constant density is

t_{\rm cool}=\frac{\tfrac32\,n\,k_BT}{n_{\rm H}^2\,\Lambda(T)}

where $n$ is the total number density of particles. The shape of $\Lambda$ follows from atomic physics. A collisional channel needs electrons or atoms with enough energy to excite the transition, so its rate carries a Boltzmann factor $e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}$: it switches on steeply near $T\sim\Delta E/k_B$ and is strong while the transition is the dominant way to lose energy. Lyα (10.2 eV) turns on near $10^4$ K, so the gas cannot cool efficiently below about 8000 K without metals or molecules; each metal ion peaks where its own transitions switch on, producing the bump near $10^5$ K and the shoulder near $10^6$ K; and at the highest temperatures the metals are stripped and only free-free emission, $\Lambda\propto T^{1/2}$, remains.

Three consequences matter for what follows. Cooling is fastest around $10^{5.3}$ K, so gas rarely lingers there: it is a transition temperature, which is why O VI absorption (Chapter 1) traces gas that is cooling or being heated. The valley around $10^{4}$ K, where the cooling function drops by orders of magnitude below its peak, is what lets warm gas persist. And metals dominate between $10^5$ and $10^7$ K, so the cooling time of hot gas depends on its enrichment; the dependence on metallicity in the figure is the reason why the first galaxies and the intergalactic medium cool so differently from the Milky Way.

Heating

In steady state the cooling is matched by heating, and in cold gas the heating comes from the interstellar radiation field and from cosmic rays rather than from compression or shocks.

The two-phase medium

Equilibrium is $n\Lambda(T)=\Gamma$ per hydrogen atom. Whether it is stable depends on how the net loss responds to a small change. Consider a parcel in pressure balance with its surroundings, so $n=P/k_BT$ and the net loss per H atom is $\mathcal L(T)=n\Lambda-\Gamma$. If the parcel cools slightly it contracts; if its net loss then rises, it keeps cooling. The condition for isobaric instability (Field 1965) is

\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial T}\right)_P<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{d\ln\Lambda}{d\ln T}<1\quad(\Gamma\ \text{independent of}\ T)

where the second form holds for heating that does not depend on temperature. Where $\Lambda$ rises more slowly than $T$, a parcel that cools becomes denser and radiates more, so it keeps cooling: there is no stable equilibrium there. With the cooling function above this happens from a few hundred K to about 6000 K, so gas can only be in stable equilibrium at two temperatures at a given pressure, a cold one and a warm one: the CNM and WNM of Chapter 1 (Field, Goldsmith and Habing 1969; Wolfire et al. 1995, 2003).

Between the pressure where the cold phase appears and the pressure where the warm phase disappears, a gas can be a mixture of CNM and WNM at the same pressure. Above the upper limit only the cold phase exists, below the lower limit only the warm one. Observationally, 21 cm absorption surveys confirm that the CNM is at 50 to 100 K, but they also find a substantial fraction of the warm gas at temperatures where the equilibrium is thermally unstable (Heiles and Troland 2003): turbulence, shocks and heating variations push gas through the unstable range faster than it can settle. Magnetic fields, thermal conduction and the Field length $\lambda_F=\sqrt{\kappa T/(n^2\Lambda)}$ then decide the size of the cold clumps that condense.

The adiabatic index of molecular gas

The sound speed and the response of gas to compression depend on the adiabatic index $\gamma=1+N_{\rm part}/c_v$, which is $5/3$ for atoms but falls when internal degrees of freedom are excited. For H₂ the rotational levels are spaced by $\sim170$ K (the $J=0\to2$ gap is 510 K) and the vibrational ladder by 6000 K, so $\gamma$ is a function of temperature and of the molecular fraction. There is a subtlety: ortho-H₂ (odd $J$, parallel proton spins) and para-H₂ (even $J$) interconvert only through collisions with protons or on grains, which is slow in the cold ISM, so the ortho-to-para ratio is usually frozen at its high-temperature value of 3:1 and the lowest transition is $J=1\to3$ in the ortho ladder.

Rigid-rotor and harmonic-oscillator model of H₂ ($\theta_{\rm rot}=87.5$ K, $\theta_{\rm vib}=6000$ K), with helium at $n_{\rm He}/n_{\rm H}=0.083$. Dissociation is not included, so the curves stop being meaningful above about 3000 K for molecular gas.

In cold molecular gas, $\gamma$ stays near $5/3$; it drops to 7/5 in the 300 to 1500 K range as rotation unfreezes, and vibration pulls it further down at higher temperatures. This matters for shock jump conditions, for the compression of gas in a collapsing core (an effective $\gamma$ above 4/3 keeps collapse stable against runaway heating) and for converting between temperature and sound speed when interpreting line widths.

Forming and destroying H₂

Molecular hydrogen is easy to destroy and hard to make. Ultraviolet light dissociates it in a few hundred years, formation takes millions, and the balance is struck only where the gas is shielded from the radiation. This chapter shows why, and how that sets where molecular gas can exist.

Formation and destruction

Two hydrogen atoms cannot simply combine in the gas phase: H₂ has no permanent dipole moment, so the radiative association H + H $\to$ H₂ + $h\nu$ is forbidden, and a collision of two atoms alone cannot remove the binding energy of 4.5 eV (it would just break the bond again). The molecule must form with the help of a third body.

On dust grains
Atoms stick to a grain, wander across its surface, and meet; the grain absorbs the binding energy. The rate per H atom is $R\,n$ with $R\approx3\times10^{-17}Z'\ {\rm cm^3\,s^{-1}}$ near 100 K (Hollenbach and McKee 1979), proportional to the dust abundance. This is the dominant route whenever the metallicity is above roughly $10^{-3}Z_\odot$.
Through H⁻
H + e⁻ $\to$ H⁻ + $h\nu$, then H⁻ + H $\to$ H₂ + e⁻. The electron acts as the catalyst, so the route needs ionization. It formed the first H₂ in the early Universe and dominates in gas without dust (Saslaw and Zipoy 1967).
Three-body
H + H + H $\to$ H₂ + H, the third atom carrying the energy. The rate goes as $n^3$, so it only matters at densities above about $10^{8}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ in primordial gas, in the collapsing cores of the first stars.

Destruction is by Lyman-Werner photons (11.2 to 13.6 eV) that excite H₂ into electronic states from which about 15% of the decays end in the dissociating continuum. In the unshielded Galactic radiation field the rate is

k_{\rm pd}=5.8\times10^{-11}\,\chi\ {\rm s^{-1}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad t_{\rm pd}\approx540\ {\rm yr}\ \chi^{-1}

with $\chi$ the intensity of the field relative to the Draine (1978) spectrum, so a molecule exposed to the interstellar field survives for only a few hundred years. Formation takes about $10^{9}\,{\rm yr}/n$, so in unshielded gas H₂ is destroyed far faster than it is made. Molecular gas exists only where the radiation has been absorbed.

Even at solar metallicity the grain route beats the free-fall time only above roughly $600\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ at 100 K, so in gas at 10 to 100 cm⁻³ the molecules take longer to form than the cloud takes to collapse unless density fluctuations and compression speed it up (clumping raises the effective rate; Glover and Mac Low 2007). At low metallicity the grain route slows in proportion to $Z$, and the H⁻ route is slower than free fall almost everywhere until the three-body route takes over near $10^{10}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$. Over much of this parameter space H₂ is therefore out of equilibrium: its abundance depends on the history of the gas, not only on its present state.

Shielding and the H I to H₂ transition

Shielding comes in two forms. Dust absorbs the Lyman-Werner photons, with an optical depth $\tau_d=\sigma_dN_{\rm H}$ and $\sigma_d\approx1.9\times10^{-21}Z'\ {\rm cm^2}$ per H atom. Self-shielding is specific to H₂: the dissociating transitions are narrow lines, so as soon as the H₂ column is large enough to make the strongest lines optically thick, the photons in those lines are absorbed by the first layer of molecules and the molecules behind it are protected. The approximation of Draine and Bertoldi (1996) is

f_{\rm shield}(N_{\rm H_2})=\frac{0.965}{(1+x/b_5)^2}+\frac{0.035}{\sqrt{1+x}}\exp\!\left[-8.5\times10^{-4}\sqrt{1+x}\right],\qquad x=\frac{N_{\rm H_2}}{5\times10^{14}\ {\rm cm^{-2}}}

with $b_5$ the Doppler parameter of the gas in units of $10^5$ cm/s (1 km/s). Because the shielding grows as the molecules accumulate, the transition is sharp: the destruction rate depends on the H₂ column, which the molecules themselves create. In steady state each layer balances formation and destruction,

R\,n\,n({\rm H\,I})=k_{\rm pd}\,\chi\,f_{\rm shield}(N_{\rm H_2})\,e^{-\tau_d}\,n({\rm H_2})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{f_{\rm H_2}}{1-f_{\rm H_2}}=\frac{2R\,n}{k_{\rm pd}\chi\,f_{\rm shield}\,e^{-\tau_d}}

where $f_{\rm H_2}=2n({\rm H_2})/n$ is the molecular fraction, and integrating this inward from an illuminated surface gives the structure of the cloud.

Three trends follow, all visible in the figure. The transition moves to larger column when the UV field is stronger or the density lower (more destruction against less formation). It moves to larger column and larger $A_V$ when the metallicity is lower, because fewer grains means slower formation and less dust shielding, so self-shielding must do all the work. And the atomic column that accumulates before the transition saturates: whatever lies deeper is molecular, so the amount of H I a cloud can hold is set by the balance, not by how much gas there is.

From a slab to a galaxy

Spatially resolved surveys of nearby galaxies (THINGS, HERACLES, BIMA SONG) show exactly this saturation: the surface density of H I levels off at about $9\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}$, and anything above that is molecular (Bigiel et al. 2008; Leroy et al. 2008). Krumholz, McKee and Tumlinson (2009) turned the slab calculation into a formula for the molecular fraction of a cloud of surface density $\Sigma$ and metallicity $Z'$, in a field of strength set by the density of the cold neutral medium:

f_{\rm H_2}=1-\frac{3}{4}\frac{s}{1+0.25\,s}\ \ (s<2),\qquad s=\frac{\ln\!\left(1+0.6\chi+0.01\chi^2\right)}{0.04\,\Sigma_{\rm comp,0}\,Z'},\quad\chi=0.77\left(1+3.1\,Z'^{0.365}\right)

with $\Sigma_{\rm comp,0}$ the surface density of the atomic-molecular complex in $M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}$ (the average, times a clumping factor $c$ of order 1 to 5). The molecular fraction is zero below a critical $\Sigma$ proportional to $1/Z'$ and rises steeply above it.

The two views agree: in a galaxy the cold gas becomes molecular where it is dense and shielded, and both the surface density of the gas and the midplane pressure of the disk are ways to say that. It also means that at low metallicity the transition needs much larger columns, and that a large part of the gas of a metal-poor galaxy can be molecular yet invisible to CO. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Carbon and CO

H₂ is invisible in cold gas, so molecular gas is measured through a trace molecule, CO. But CO is more fragile than H₂, and the gas it misses depends on the dust, the radiation field and the metallicity. This chapter follows carbon from the surface of a cloud inward and asks what the CO lines really measure.

Layers of a photodissociation region

A cloud lit from outside is a photodissociation region (PDR; Tielens and Hollenbach 1985; Hollenbach and Tielens 1999). The radiation field is absorbed from the surface inward by dust, by the Lyman-Werner lines of H₂, and by the bands that dissociate CO, so the chemistry is stratified: each species survives only below the depth where the radiation that destroys it has been attenuated.

  • Surface. Hydrogen is atomic, since H₂ is photodissociated faster than it forms. Carbon is C⁺: its ionization threshold (11.26 eV) lies below the hydrogen one, so FUV photons ionize it throughout the neutral gas.
  • H₂ front. After the H₂ column becomes self-shielding (Sect. Shielding and the H I to H₂ transition), hydrogen turns molecular. Carbon is still ionized. In this layer the gas is molecular but has no CO.
  • C⁺/C and CO fronts. Dust attenuates the 11 eV photons that ionize carbon, and CO is protected by dust and by the H₂ lines that overlap its dissociating bands. In diffuse gas CO forms while most of the carbon is still C⁺; in dense, strongly shielded gas a thin layer of neutral carbon sits between C⁺ and CO. Deeper than the CO front, carbon is in CO and the gas cools through its rotational lines.

The C⁺/C balance is the simplest to write down. With electrons supplied by carbon itself, photoionization of C against radiative recombination of C⁺ gives an ionized fraction $f_i$ from $\Gamma_{\rm C}(1-f_i)=\alpha_{\rm C}\,n\,x_{\rm C}Z'\,f_i^2$, where $\Gamma_{\rm C}=3.1\times10^{-10}\chi\,e^{-3A_V}\ {\rm s^{-1}}$ and $x_{\rm C}=1.4\times10^{-4}$ is the gas-phase carbon abundance. The transition therefore sits where $\chi\,e^{-3A_V}\sim n\,Z'$: at about $A_V\approx\tfrac13\ln(\chi/nZ')$ plus a constant, deeper in a stronger field and shallower in denser gas.

CO and dark gas

Between the H₂ front and the CO front the gas is molecular but has no CO to emit: it is CO-dark molecular gas. It is not exotic: a cloud illuminated on both sides has a dark layer on each side, and the smaller or the more metal-poor the cloud, the larger the dark layer is compared to the CO-bright core. It was first inferred as excess gamma-ray emission and excess dust emission above what CO and H I predicted (Grenier et al. 2005; Planck Collaboration 2011), and then measured through [C II] 158 μm (Langer et al. 2014), which finds that of order 30% of the molecular gas in the Milky Way is CO-dark. Theory and models agree (Wolfire, Hollenbach and McKee 2010).

The conversion factor

CO lines are optically thick, so their brightness does not count molecules. That CO nevertheless gives masses at all rests on a statistical argument about clouds (Dickman, Snell and Schloerb 1986). The integrated intensity $W_{\rm CO}=\int T_B\,dv$ of a virialized cloud of radius $R$, velocity dispersion $\sigma$ and brightness temperature $T_B$ goes as $R^2\,T_B\,\sigma$, while its virial mass goes as $\sigma^2R$, so the mass per unit CO luminosity is

\alpha_{\rm CO}\equiv\frac{M}{L_{\rm CO}}\ \propto\ \frac{\sigma}{R\,T_B}\ \propto\ \frac{n^{1/2}}{T_B}

where the last step uses $\sigma^2\propto GM/R\propto G\rho R^2$. The conversion factor depends on the mean density and the brightness temperature of the CO-emitting gas: constant clouds, constant factor. Observations of the Milky Way disk give $X_{\rm CO}=N({\rm H_2})/W_{\rm CO}\approx2\times10^{20}\ {\rm cm^{-2}\,(K\,km\,s^{-1})^{-1}}$, equivalent to $\alpha_{\rm CO}\approx4.3\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}\,({\rm K\,km\,s^{-1}})^{-1}$ with helium included, with a scatter of about 30% (Bolatto, Wolfire and Leroy 2013). The factor changes when the assumptions fail.

The CO ladder and line ratios

CO is a linear rotor with levels at $E_J/k_B=2.77\,J(J+1)$ K and transitions at $\nu_J=115.27\,J$ GHz (J to J−1). Each line needs gas dense enough to excite the upper level collisionally faster than it decays radiatively, the critical density $n_{\rm crit}=A_{J,J-1}/\gamma_{\rm coll}$, which rises as $J^4$ and is a few $10^3\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ for 1-0. Above $n_{\rm crit}$ the levels are thermalized at the gas temperature; below it the line is fainter than its LTE value by roughly $n/n_{\rm crit}$. The ladder of line fluxes against $J$ (the spectral line energy distribution, SLED) is therefore a thermometer and a densitometer.

An optically thin CO ladder interactive

Flux per line, in units of the 1-0 line, in the optically thin limit: $S_J\propto N_J\,A_{J,J-1}\,\nu_J$ with $N_J\propto(2J+1)e^{-E_J/k_BT}$, reduced below the critical density by $[1+n_{\rm crit}(J)/n]^{-1}$ (two-level approximation, $A_{10}=7.2\times10^{-8}\ {\rm s^{-1}}$, $\gamma_{\rm coll}=3\times10^{-11}\ {\rm cm^3\,s^{-1}}$). Real lines are partly optically thick and radiative trapping lowers the effective critical density, so use it for trends, not for numbers.

Observed ratios follow the same logic. In nearby galaxy disks the ratio of CO 2-1 to 1-0 brightness temperature is about 0.6 to 0.7 (Leroy et al. 2013), and approaches 1 in dense, warm gas where both lines are thermalized and optically thick. The ratio $^{12}$CO/$^{13}$CO is a few to ten in clouds, because the common isotopologue is optically thick and its rare partner is not, and it is used to measure the optical depth and hence the column. Combined with [C II], [C I] and the dust continuum, the line ratios are what let observers solve for the density, temperature and radiation field of a PDR.

Chemical networks

A chemical network is a list of species and the reactions that connect them, with a rate for each. Writing the rates down is the easy part. The difficulty is that the rates span more than twenty orders of magnitude, so some processes are in equilibrium on any timescale of interest and others never are.

The species

Before the reactions, the actors. The cold ISM contains atoms and ions, a few small molecules that carry most of the information, and, in the form of PAHs, large molecules that sit between gas and dust. Their geometry and electronic structure decide what they can do: whether a molecule has a permanent dipole moment (CO does, H₂ does not), how its rotational levels are spaced (set by its moment of inertia, so light molecules such as CH⁺ have lines at much higher frequency than CO), and which transitions are allowed. The curves illustrate why chemistry is a stiff problem. Radiative recombination slowly falls with temperature while the endothermic reaction rises by twenty orders of magnitude between 100 and 3000 K; the associative detachment rate is constant and large, so H⁻ is destroyed the instant it forms and the slow attachment step sets the pace. A network with all of these has to evolve species whose lifetimes differ by factors of $10^{10}$ or more, which is why it is solved as a set of stiff equations (Sect. Reduced networks).

Ionization and cosmic rays

In regions where the UV is shielded, the ionization that drives the chemistry comes from cosmic rays. The chain is short and fast:

{\rm H_2}+{\rm CR}\to{\rm H_2^+}+e^-,\qquad {\rm H_2^+}+{\rm H_2}\to{\rm H_3^+}+{\rm H},\qquad {\rm H_3^+}+X\to{\rm HX^+}+{\rm H_2}

H₃⁺ is the proton donor of cold gas: it transfers its extra proton to any neutral with a higher proton affinity than H₂, including CO, O and C, and starts the ion-molecule chains that make OH, H₂O and the hydrocarbons. The second step is so fast ($\sim2\times10^{-9}\ {\rm cm^3\,s^{-1}}$) that every primary ionization of H₂ becomes an H₃⁺ almost immediately, so the abundance of H₃⁺ in diffuse molecular clouds measures $\zeta$ directly: observations of H₃⁺ absorption give $\zeta_{\rm H}\sim(1\text{ to }5)\times10^{-16}\ {\rm s^{-1}}$ along many sight lines (McCall et al. 2003; Indriolo and McCall 2012), several times higher than the older value of $10^{-17}\ {\rm s^{-1}}$ and with a large scatter. It also sets the heating of dense gas (Sect. Heating) and the ionization fraction, which controls how well magnetic fields couple to the gas in cores.

Chemical against dynamical timescales

The key question about any process in a network is whether it is faster than the gas changes. If the timescale of a reaction is much shorter than the dynamical (free-fall) time, the species it controls can be treated as being in equilibrium with the local conditions, and can be removed from the network with an algebraic relation. If it is longer, the abundance depends on the history of the gas and has to be followed in time.

In dense gas nearly everything is faster than free fall, except the formation of H₂ and CO at low metallicity; at the density of the diffuse ISM the formation of H₂ is slower than free fall and its abundance is only in equilibrium in gas that has been dense for a long time. This is the practical meaning of "non-equilibrium chemistry".

Out of equilibrium: H₂ in a cloud that is suddenly dense

The simplest non-equilibrium problem is a one-zone cloud at fixed density, column and field that begins atomic (or molecular) and relaxes. With $f=2n({\rm H_2})/n$, one-zone shielding at column $N_{\rm H}$, and the rates above,

\frac{df}{dt}=2\,R\,n\,(1-f)-k_{\rm pd}\,f,\qquad k_{\rm pd}=k_{\rm pd}\big(\chi,\ N_{\rm H_2}=\tfrac12fN_{\rm H},\ N_{\rm H},\ Z'\big)

Because $k_{\rm pd}$ falls as $f$ rises (self-shielding), the equation can have more than one steady state: a gas with an atomic start can stay atomic while the same gas with a molecular start stays molecular. This bistability is a known feature of one-zone self-shielding models and makes the final state depend on the history.

Reduced networks

Full databases of interstellar chemistry contain thousands of reactions among hundreds of species (the UMIST database, McElroy et al. 2013; KIDA, Wakelam et al. 2012). Modelling a cloud or a galaxy needs far fewer. The reduction works for physical reasons:

  • Elements and charge are conserved. Fixing the total of each element removes one equation each, and charge neutrality fixes the electron abundance.
  • Most species are fast intermediates. They have a short lifetime against the next reaction in the chain, so their abundance is set algebraically by the slow steps before and after them (the bottleneck or rate-limiting step), and they need not be followed. CO formation is one such chain, controlled by the C⁺ + H₂ step in diffuse gas.
  • Only the heaviest contributors to cooling and heating need to be kept. What a simulation needs is H, H⁺, H₂, e⁻, C⁺, C, CO and a few oxygen species, because those carry the cooling and the shielding.

Molecular gas and star formation

Stars form in molecular gas, so the molecular content of a galaxy sets how fast it makes stars. Maps of nearby galaxies show how tight this link is, and what it implies for the efficiency of the process.

The Kennicutt-Schmidt relation

Schmidt (1959) proposed that the star formation rate density follows a power of the gas density. Kennicutt (1998), using the disk-averaged surface densities of spirals and starbursts, found

\Sigma_{\rm SFR}=2.5\times10^{-4}\left(\frac{\Sigma_{\rm gas}}{1\ M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}}\right)^{1.4}\ M_\odot\,{\rm yr^{-1}\,kpc^{-2}}

for the total (atomic plus molecular) gas, over five orders of magnitude. Resolved observations of nearby galaxies sharpened the picture: the star formation rate surface density correlates with the molecular gas, not with H I, and the relation for H₂ is close to linear, $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}\propto\Sigma_{\rm H_2}^{1.0\pm0.2}$, with a molecular gas depletion time $t_{\rm dep}=\Sigma_{\rm H_2}/\Sigma_{\rm SFR}\approx2$ Gyr, almost the same in all the spiral disks studied (Bigiel et al. 2008; Leroy et al. 2008, 2013). The steeper total-gas law arises because the molecular fraction itself rises with $\Sigma$ (Chapter 3).

Depletion times and the efficiency per free-fall time

The depletion time is the natural observable. To interpret it, compare it with the timescale on which gas can collapse, the free-fall time $t_{\rm ff}\propto\rho^{-1/2}$, through the efficiency per free-fall time

\varepsilon_{\rm ff}=\frac{t_{\rm ff}}{t_{\rm dep}}=\frac{\dot M_\star\,t_{\rm ff}}{M_{\rm gas}}

For the cloud scales at which stars form ($n\sim10^{2}$ to $10^{3}\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$) the free-fall time is 1 to 4 Myr, and a depletion time of 2 Gyr gives $\varepsilon_{\rm ff}$ of a few $10^{-3}$ for the whole molecular reservoir; measured on the dense gas that forms stars it is about 0.01 (Krumholz and Tan 2007). Two points matter. First, the efficiency is low: a cloud forms stars far more slowly than free fall, so something supports it, or removes gas, for many dynamical times (turbulence and feedback are the usual candidates; see the Stellar particles in AthenaPK page). Second, it is roughly universal across a wide range of environments, which suggests that the physics that regulates it is local to the clouds, not set by the galaxy (the figure above lets you read $\varepsilon_{\rm ff}$ for any depletion time and density).

Cloud scaling relations

Giant molecular clouds obey scaling relations first found by Larson (1981) and refined with CO surveys of the Milky Way (Solomon et al. 1987; Heyer et al. 2009):

\sigma\simeq0.72\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}}\left(\frac{R}{1\ {\rm pc}}\right)^{0.5},\qquad \Sigma=\frac{M}{\pi R^2}\sim100\text{ to }200\ M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}

The first says that clouds have supersonic internal motions that grow with scale as in a turbulent cascade; the second that their surface density is nearly constant, so mass goes as $R^2$. Together they place clouds near virial equilibrium: with $M=\pi R^2\Sigma$, the virial parameter $\alpha_{\rm vir}=5\sigma^2R/(GM)$ is unity for $\sigma^2=\pi G\Sigma R/5$, and with $\Sigma\approx190\,M_\odot\,{\rm pc^{-2}}$ that gives $\sigma\approx0.72\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}(R/{\rm pc})^{1/2}$: the same line. Whether this is virial balance or the result of a turbulent cascade of the same shape is a long-standing debate; bound or not, the relation is the observational counterpart of the virial test used in the Stellar particles in AthenaPK page.

Cold gas in cluster cores

The hot gas at the centres of many galaxy clusters should cool and flow inwards, forming stars at hundreds of solar masses per year. It does not. Yet filaments of $10^4$ K gas and large reservoirs of molecular gas are there, at the pressure of the hot gas around them. This is the thermal instability of Chapter 2, in a different regime.

Cool cores

The intracluster medium (ICM) is a hot, tenuous plasma at the virial temperature of the cluster, 1 to 10 keV, which radiates by thermal bremsstrahlung and metal lines in X-rays (Chapter 1). Its thermodynamic state is described by the entropy variable $K=k_BT\,n_e^{-2/3}$, which sets the buoyancy of the gas, and by the cooling time of Chapter 2, here dominated by bremsstrahlung and metals:

t_{\rm cool}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{n\,k_BT}{n_en_{\rm H}\Lambda(T)}\approx0.5\ {\rm Gyr}\left(\frac{k_BT}{3\ {\rm keV}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{n_e}{0.1\ {\rm cm^{-3}}}\right)^{-1}

Averaged over the cluster, the cooling time exceeds the age of the Universe. But in the central tens of kpc of many clusters the gas density rises steeply and the cooling time drops to well below a Gyr: a cool core. The entropy profiles of clusters follow a floor-plus-power-law form $K(r)=K_0+K_{100}(r/100\ {\rm kpc})^{1.1}$ with $K_{100}\approx150\ {\rm keV\,cm^2}$, and the central entropy $K_0$ divides clusters into a cool-core group ($K_0\lesssim30$ to 50 keV cm²) and a non-cool-core group (Cavagnolo et al. 2009). If nothing heated the gas, it would cool and flow inwards at a rate fixed by the luminosity it radiates,

\dot M_{\rm cf}=\frac{2}{5}\,\frac{\mu m_p}{k_BT}\,L_{\rm cool}\ \approx\ 130\ M_\odot\,{\rm yr^{-1}}\left(\frac{L_{\rm cool}}{10^{44}\ {\rm erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)\left(\frac{k_BT}{3\ {\rm keV}}\right)^{-1}

(Fabian 1994), which for the brightest cool cores reaches hundreds to a thousand $M_\odot$ per year. The deficit is the cooling-flow problem: X-ray spectra show almost no emission lines from gas below about a third of the cluster temperature (Peterson et al. 2003), and the star formation rates and cold gas masses of the central galaxies correspond to a small fraction, a few to ten per cent at most, of $\dot M_{\rm cf}$.

Thermal instability in a stratified atmosphere

In the ISM any isobaric perturbation in the unstable range of temperatures grows. A cluster atmosphere is different: it is stratified in a gravitational field, so gas displaced downward finds itself denser than its surroundings and falls, and gas displaced upward floats back. A perturbation therefore oscillates at the buoyancy (Brunt-Väisälä) frequency, which for the entropy profiles above gives a period of the order of the free-fall time. Whether it can grow depends on the comparison of the cooling time with the free-fall time: if $t_{\rm cool}\gg t_{\rm ff}$ the oscillation is restored faster than the gas cools and the atmosphere is stable; if $t_{\rm cool}\lesssim t_{\rm ff}$ the displaced gas cools before it rises back, and the perturbation condenses (McCourt et al. 2012).

t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{\frac{2r}{g}}=\frac{r}{\sigma}\ \ \text{(isothermal potential)},\qquad \text{condensation}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \min\left(\frac{t_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm ff}}\right)\lesssim1\ \text{to}\ 10

Numerical simulations with heating from the central black hole find that condensation, precipitation, sets in when $\min(t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff})\lesssim10$ (Sharma et al. 2012; Voit et al. 2015): the nonlinear growth is faster than the linear estimate, and the atmosphere keeps itself near that threshold by feedback. Observations support it: clusters with cold gas and Hα emission have a minimum $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ below roughly 10 to 30, and those with larger values do not (Voit et al. 2015). This is the same kind of threshold that appeared as the entropy criterion $K_0<30\ {\rm keV\,cm^2}$ for Hα emission in Cavagnolo et al. (2008). The ratio in the figure above is where to look: with the low central entropy and the central potential of the defaults it reaches the observed range at about 10 kpc, and with a higher central entropy it stays far above it.

Hα filaments and molecular gas

The cold gas of cool cores appears as filaments hundreds of pc wide and up to tens of kpc long around the central galaxy, in two forms that are co-spatial: ionized gas at $10^4$ K seen in Hα and forbidden lines, and molecular gas seen in CO. The best studied is the Perseus cluster, around NGC 1275, whose Hα filaments (Conselice et al. 2001; Fabian et al. 2003) lie along cavities inflated by the radio jets. Their properties are striking.

  • Mass. Molecular gas masses of $10^{9}$ to $10^{11}\ M_\odot$ in the most luminous cool cores (Edge 2001; Salomé and Combes 2003; Russell et al. 2017), of order $10^{10}\ M_\odot$ in Perseus; the ionized gas is a thousand times lighter, so most of the cold mass is molecular.
  • Co-spatial and co-moving. CO and Hα follow the same filaments and have similar velocities, so the two phases are physically linked (Salomé et al. 2011; Lim et al. 2008).
  • Slow. Velocities of 100 to 300 km s⁻¹, below the stellar velocity dispersion of the central galaxy, and in ALMA observations of several systems the cold clouds move too slowly to be on free-fall orbits, pointing to infall that is slowed down (Russell et al. 2016).
  • Pressure-confined. At $P/k_B\sim10^6$ to $10^7\ {\rm K\,cm^{-3}}$, the pressure of the hot gas, so the filaments are cold clumps in the same atmosphere (Chapter 1, figure of the phases).
  • Heated by something. The Hα luminosity is too large for photoionization by the stars alone, and the ratios of lines such as [N II]/Hα and the rotational H₂ lines show excitation by particles from the hot gas, cosmic rays or turbulent mixing layers (Ferland et al. 2009; Fabian et al. 2011).

These facts fit precipitation: cold gas condenses out of the hot atmosphere where $t_{\rm cool}/t_{\rm ff}$ is small, falls in on slow, chaotic orbits and feeds the central black hole, whose feedback then heats the atmosphere and limits further condensation (Gaspari et al. 2013; Voit et al. 2015).

Dust in a hot plasma

Dust is expected to be destroyed in the hot ICM: ions at $10^7$ to $10^8$ K hit grains and knock atoms off their surfaces, sputtering them. For grains of radius $a$ in gas of hydrogen density $n_{\rm H}$ the standard fit (Draine and Salpeter 1979; Tsai and Mathews 1995) is

\frac{da}{dt}\approx-3.2\times10^{-18}\,n_{\rm H}\left[\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{2.5}+1\right]^{-1}\ {\rm cm\,s^{-1}},\qquad T_0=2\times10^6\ {\rm K}

The destruction time is $t_{\rm sp}=a/|da/dt|$, which for $a=0.1\ \mu$m and $T>10^6$ K is about $10^{5}\,{\rm yr}\,(n_{\rm H}/{\rm cm^{-3}})^{-1}$. For the ICM density of $0.01$ to $0.1\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ that is of order $10^6$ to $10^7$ yr, short against the age of the cluster, so grains in the hot gas do not survive. The dust seen with Herschel and Spitzer in filaments of cool cores must be protected inside the cold gas, which is shielded from the hot plasma by its own surface layers, or injected there by the central galaxy (see also the Dust physics page for the physics of grain destruction).

Cooling and heating

The cooling-flow problem is a heating problem: something has to put back in the gas the energy it radiates. The leading candidate is the supermassive black hole of the central galaxy, whose jets inflate cavities in the hot gas, visible as holes in X-ray images, that rise and do work on the gas. The enthalpy of a cavity of volume $V$ in gas of pressure $P$ is $4PV$ (for a relativistic fluid), and dividing by the age $t$ gives the mechanical power, which can be compared with the cooling luminosity. In a large sample of clusters, groups and ellipticals the two are comparable (Bîrzan et al. 2004; Rafferty et al. 2006; McNamara and Nulsen 2007). Together with the observation that the cooling gas is accreted by the black hole as cold, chaotic clumps, this closes a feedback loop: cooling feeds the black hole, and the black hole heats the gas. The physics of the engine itself is in the Active galactic nuclei page.
