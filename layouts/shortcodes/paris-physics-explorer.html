History of science

Paris and its physics, 1200–2026

The University of Paris is one of the oldest universities in the world, yet for most of its history it was not where Parisian physics was done. Physics happened in a crowd of institutions around it: the Collège de France, the Académie des sciences and the Observatoire under the kings; the grandes écoles after the Revolution; the Institut du Radium, the CNRS and the CEA in the twentieth century; and, since the 1960s, joint laboratories that belong to several bodies at once. The university itself was abolished in 1793, rebuilt in 1896, split into thirteen in 1970, and has been reassembled into a few large universities since 2018.

This page follows that story from a physicist’s point of view, with an emphasis on astrophysics, nuclear and particle physics, and cosmic rays. Its centerpiece is an atlas: a timeline of about seventy institutions, laboratories and machines, linked to a map that you can play forward in time. Every chapter opens with a short box that says where it is going, and a ✦ marks an interactive figure.

A bar on the timeline runs from a founding to a closure or a merger. Many institutions changed name, status and address several times, so a single bar simplifies, and the entry for each one says what it hides. Dates marked “c.” are approximate, map positions are good to a few hundred meters, and the references say which claims were checked and which were written from memory.

A medieval university, and physics outside it (1200–1793)

In this chapterWhat “physics” meant in a medieval faculty of arts, the Parisian masters who asked modern questions about motion, and why the physics of the Scientific Revolution in Paris was done at royal institutions rather than at the university.

Natural philosophy in the faculty of arts

In this sectionThe university as a guild of masters, Aristotle as the syllabus, the condemnation of 1277, and Buridan’s and Oresme’s ideas about motion.

The University of Paris was not founded on a given day. It grew, in the late twelfth century, out of the schools of Notre-Dame and of the Left Bank, as a guild (universitas) of masters and students that obtained a royal privilege in 1200, statutes in 1215 and its autonomy from the bishop through the papal bull Parens scientiarum of 1231. It had four faculties: arts, theology, canon law and medicine. Students lived in colleges, among them the Collège de Sorbonne (1257), whose name came to stand for the whole university, and the Collège de Navarre (1305).

“Physics” was the natural philosophy of Aristotle, read in the faculty of arts from his Physics and On the Heavens. It was taught through commentaries and disputed questions, without measurements and without laboratories. In 1277 the bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned 219 propositions, several of which limited what God could do, such as the claim that God could not move the outermost heavens in a straight line because that would leave a vacuum. Pierre Duhem argued in the early twentieth century that this condemnation set natural philosophy free and marked the birth of modern science. Most historians now find that overstated, but it did make it legitimate to reason about a vacuum and about other worlds as hypotheses.

The fourteenth-century Parisian masters then asked questions that look strikingly modern. Jean Buridan, twice rector of the university, explained why a thrown stone keeps moving: the thrower gives it an impetus, proportional to its quantity of matter and its speed, which lasts until air resistance and weight wear it down. With no resistance in the heavens, the impetus given at the Creation could keep the spheres turning forever, with no need for angels to push them. Impetus is not momentum, but it is the closest medieval idea to it. Nicole Oresme, grand master of the Collège de Navarre, drew the intensity of a quality against time as a figure, which let him show geometrically that a uniformly accelerated body covers the same distance as one moving at its mean speed. In his Livre du ciel et du monde (1377), written in French for Charles V, he argued that no observation or reason can prove that the Earth does not turn daily on its axis, before concluding, as was expected, that it does not.

The punchline. The Parisian masters of the fourteenth century asked modern questions about motion, but in a faculty with no instruments and a fixed syllabus. Physics remained a branch of philosophy at the university for three more centuries.

The royal institutions take over (1530–1793)

In this sectionThe Collège royal, the Jardin du roi, the Académie and the Observatoire: where the physics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was actually done in Paris, up to the Revolution.

The kings did not reform the university. They built around it. François I created the lecteurs royaux in 1530, the future Collège de France: free public lectures, in subjects the university ignored, with no exams and no degrees. Louis XIII founded the Jardin du roi in 1635. Colbert then created the Académie royale des sciences in 1666, with paid academicians and a budget for experiments, and the Observatoire in 1667, run for four generations by the Cassini family.

The Observatory produced the first great result in Parisian physics. In 1676 Ole Rømer, working there, noticed that the eclipses of Jupiter’s moon Io came later when the Earth moved away from Jupiter and earlier when it moved closer, and he predicted that an eclipse in November 1676 would be about ten minutes late. The delay is the time light takes to cross the extra distance: light has a finite speed. Combined with the size of the Earth’s orbit, Rømer’s estimate gave Huygens about 220,000 km/s. The Observatory also defined the Paris meridian, surveyed France, and supplied the measurement of the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona (1792–1798) on which the metre was based. That work later led to the international bureau of weights and measures, the BIPM at Sèvres.

The Revolution swept all of this away. The Convention abolished the academies in August 1793 and the universities in September 1793. The Académie came back in 1795 as a class of the new Institut national, but the University of Paris did not.

The punchline. By 1793 the university that had made Paris famous had played almost no part in the Scientific Revolution: the physics of the Enlightenment was made at the Académie and the Observatoire.

Schools, a faculty and the age of light (1794–1895)

In this chapterThe grandes écoles founded by the Revolution, a faculty of sciences built for teaching, a century of discoveries in optics, heat and electromagnetism, and the birth of astrophysics at Meudon.

The grandes écoles and the faculty of sciences

In this sectionWhy France ended up with two parallel higher-education systems, and what that meant for where research was done.

The Revolution needed engineers, officers and teachers fast, and it created schools rather than a university. The École polytechnique (1794) trained engineers for the state, with Monge, Lagrange and Fourier among its teachers; it moved in 1805 into the buildings of the old Collège de Navarre on the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. The École normale supérieure (first opened in 1795, refounded in 1808, rue d’Ulm from 1847) trained teachers, and the Conservatoire des arts et métiers (1794) kept machines and instruments. Napoleon then organized an Imperial University in 1808, in which the Faculté des sciences de Paris, with Biot and Gay-Lussac among its professors, granted degrees and taught, but had almost no budget for research.

This produced a lasting split. The schools selected and trained the elite; the faculties examined; and research was done wherever a professor could find a laboratory, often in several institutions at once. A physicist could teach at Polytechnique, hold a chair at the Collège de France, sit at the Académie and work at the Observatory.

Astronomy splits in two, and the university returns

In this sectionPositional astronomy at the Observatoire, physical astronomy at Meudon, and the re-creation of the University of Paris in 1896.

The Paris Observatory under Le Verrier remained devoted to positions and orbits, and from 1887 it hosted the international Carte du Ciel, a photographic map of the whole sky. The new physical astronomy, which uses spectroscopy and photography to ask what stars and the Sun are made of, found its home elsewhere: in 1876 Jules Janssen founded the observatory of Meudon, on the hill south-west of Paris, after observing the solar prominences spectroscopically during the eclipse of 1868. Meudon was joined to the Paris Observatory in 1926. There, in 1930, Bernard Lyot built the coronagraph, which hides the disk of the Sun to observe its corona without an eclipse.

Meanwhile the state rebuilt the university. The law of 1896 regrouped the Paris faculties into a University of Paris again, and the new Sorbonne (1885–1901) gave the Faculté des sciences laboratories at last. It was in Lippmann’s laboratory there that Maria Skłodowska started her research in 1894.

Radioactivity in the Latin Quarter (1896–1939)

In this chapterForty years in which a few streets of the Latin Quarter discovered radioactivity, artificial radioactivity and the neutron chain reaction, confirmed atoms, proposed matter waves and found cosmic-ray air showers, while France built a national research system.

Within a kilometer and a half of the Panthéon

In this sectionFrom Becquerel’s plates to the Institut du Radium, and how close together it all happened.

In February and March 1896 Henri Becquerel, who held the physics chair of the Muséum after his father and grandfather, found that uranium salts fog a photographic plate with no sunlight at all: a new, spontaneous radiation. Two years later Marie and Pierre Curie, working in a shed of the École de physique et chimie (ESPCI), where Pierre taught, measured the ionization produced by minerals with an electrometer built around the piezoelectric quartz that Pierre and his brother Jacques had discovered. Pitchblende was more active than its uranium could explain, and the excess led them to polonium (July 1898) and radium (December 1898, with Gustave Bémont). The 1903 Nobel Prize in physics went to all three. Pierre got a Sorbonne chair in 1904; after his death in 1906, Marie succeeded him and became the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. The university and the Institut Pasteur then built the Institut du Radium (agreed 1909, finished 1914), with a physics and chemistry laboratory directed by Marie Curie and a biology laboratory directed by Claudius Regaud.

The Muséum, ESPCI, the École normale, the Institut du Radium, the Sorbonne, the Collège de France, Polytechnique and the Observatoire all lie within about 1.5 km of the Panthéon, a twenty-minute walk.

Atoms, waves and a national research system

In this sectionPerrin’s atoms, de Broglie’s waves, and how the Paris physicists built the funding bodies that became the CNRS.

Jean Perrin, at the Faculté des sciences, measured in 1908 how small grains in suspension settle under gravity and jiggle under molecular impacts, as Einstein’s theory of Brownian motion predicted, and obtained Avogadro’s number from them. That ended the debate on the reality of atoms (Nobel Prize 1926). Paul Langevin, at the Collège de France from 1909 and later director of ESPCI, brought relativity and the new quantum theory to France. In 1924 Louis de Broglie defended at the Faculté a thesis proposing that every particle has a wave of wavelength $h/p$ attached to it, with $h$ Planck’s constant and $p$ the momentum (Nobel Prize 1929). From 1928 the Institut Henri Poincaré, funded by the Rockefeller Foundation and Edmond de Rothschild, gave theoretical physics a building of its own.

Physicists also built the institutions that pay for research. After the Office national des recherches scientifiques (1922, with its laboratories at Bellevue) and a national research fund in the 1930s, the Popular Front government made Irène Joliot-Curie, then Jean Perrin, under-secretary of state for scientific research in 1936. That year it also founded the Institut d’astrophysique de Paris, whose building next to the Observatoire was finished only in 1952. On 19 October 1939 the CNRS was created, with full-time research positions that did not depend on teaching.

From artificial radioactivity to the chain reaction

In this sectionThe Joliot-Curies’ near-miss with the neutron, their discovery of artificial radioactivity, and the 1939 measurement that made a reactor possible.

At the Institut du Radium, Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot had the strongest polonium sources in the world, which made them leaders in nuclear physics. In early 1932 they found that the penetrating radiation that Bothe had seen coming from beryllium knocks protons out of paraffin. They took it for gamma rays; weeks later Chadwick showed it was a neutral particle, the neutron. In January 1934 they bombarded aluminum with alpha particles and found that the target kept emitting positrons after the source was removed: they had made a new, radioactive isotope of phosphorus,

²⁷Al + α → ³⁰P + n,    then    ³⁰P → ³⁰Si + e⁺ + ν

the first artificial radioactivity (Nobel Prize in chemistry 1935). Here α is a helium nucleus, n a neutron, e⁺ a positron and ν a neutrino; ³⁰P has a half-life of about 2.5 minutes. Frédéric Joliot moved in 1937 to the Collège de France, where he built one of the first cyclotrons in Europe, and opened a second laboratory at Ivry. In the spring of 1939, with Hans von Halban and Lew Kowarski, he showed that each fission of uranium releases more than one neutron, enough in principle for a self-sustaining chain reaction, and the group filed patents on a reactor. In 1940 Halban and Kowarski left for Britain with the world’s stock of heavy water.

The punchline. In 1939, the physics that would make nuclear reactors possible was being done a few hundred meters from the medieval colleges where Buridan and Oresme had argued about motion.

Cosmic rays from Paris

In this sectionLeprince-Ringuet’s cosmic-ray laboratory at Polytechnique, and how Pierre Auger’s fast coincidence counters revealed showers of particles from a single cosmic ray. A small interactive figure shows why the speed of the electronics mattered.

In 1936 Louis Leprince-Ringuet became professor at Polytechnique and founded its first laboratory, devoted to cosmic rays: a cloud chamber in a strong magnet measures the momentum of each charged particle from the curvature of its track. The group took its detectors to altitude, at Largentière in the Alps and at the Pic du Midi in the Pyrenees, where the flux is larger. In 1944 Leprince-Ringuet and Michel Lhéritier measured a particle of about a thousand electron masses, now read as an early sighting of what was later called the charged K meson.

At the same time Pierre Auger, professor at the Faculté des sciences, was studying coincidences between Geiger counters with Roland Maze, whose electronics could tell whether two pulses arrived within a few microseconds. In 1938 they found that counters placed several meters, then tens and hundreds of meters apart, at the Jungfraujoch at 3,450 m, still fired together far more often than chance allowed. Each coincidence was a shower of secondary particles produced in the atmosphere by a single primary cosmic ray. Auger estimated that the primaries carried at least $10^{15}$ eV, a million times more than any radioactive source. Rossi in 1934, and Kolhörster and others in 1938, had seen related effects, but Auger’s group showed how extensive the showers are. The largest cosmic-ray observatory in the world, in Argentina, bears his name.

Accidental and real coincidences interactive

Two counters each fire at a rate $R$ from unrelated particles. Two independent pulses fall within the resolving time $\tau$ of each other by chance at the accidental rate $R_{\rm acc}=2\tau R^2$. A shower crossing both counters gives real coincidences at a rate $R_{\rm s}$, set here by hand (per hour). Counters were moved from a few meters to hundreds of meters apart. The bar chart compares the two over one hour of counting, on a logarithmic scale; a signal is convincing when $R_{\rm s}$ exceeds $R_{\rm acc}$ by several times the statistical fluctuation $\sqrt{N_{\rm acc}}$, where $N$ is a number of counts. The rates are illustrative, not Auger’s.

The punchline. The accidental rate falls in proportion to the resolving time: a circuit a hundred times faster cuts the chance coincidences a hundredfold. Faster electronics are what turned a small excess into the discovery of air showers.

Big science moves south (1945–1970)

In this chapterHow the CEA and the faculty of sciences moved nuclear and particle physics out of Paris to Châtillon, Saclay and Orsay, and what was built there: reactors, a synchrocyclotron, a large linear accelerator and some of the first electron-positron colliders.

The CEA: Châtillon and Saclay

In this sectionFrance’s atomic energy commission, its first reactor and the Saclay center.

The Commissariat à l’énergie atomique was created in October 1945, with Frédéric Joliot as high commissioner. Its first laboratory was the Fort de Châtillon, at Fontenay-aux-Roses, where ZOÉ, the first French reactor (heavy water and uranium oxide, at almost zero power), went critical on 15 December 1948. Joliot was dismissed in 1950 for political reasons, and Francis Perrin, Jean Perrin’s son, held the post from 1951 to 1970. A much larger center opened at Saclay, on the plateau south-west of Paris, from 1952: research reactors, the Saturne proton synchrotron from 1958, a theory service, astrophysics from the 1960s, and the engineering that built large detectors. The heavy-liquid bubble chamber Gargamelle, designed and built at Saclay and run at CERN, gave the first evidence of weak neutral currents in 1973, in a collaboration led by André Lagarrigue of Orsay.

The Orsay campus

In this sectionWhy the Faculty of Sciences built a campus 25 km from the Sorbonne, and the laboratories that grew there.

Accelerators need hectares of land, shielding and electric power, none of which the Latin Quarter could offer, and the new faculty buildings at the Halle aux vins (Jussieu) were years late. Irène Joliot-Curie therefore started a nuclear physics laboratory on land at Orsay in 1956; she died that year, and Frédéric Joliot, himself ill, completed it. The first physicists arrived in the spring of 1957, the synchrocyclotron delivered its first beam in June 1958, and Joliot died in August. The cyclotron of the Collège de France was moved there too. This was the Institut de physique nucléaire.

Yves Rocard’s physics laboratory at the École normale did the same for electrons: from 1955 it built the Laboratoire de l’accélérateur linéaire (LAL) at Orsay, around a linear accelerator that reached 2.3 GeV by 1968. The ENS theorists followed in 1959–60 (LPT), solid-state physicists founded the Laboratoire de physique des solides (1959), and the mass spectrometry and nuclear spectroscopy groups formed the CSNSM (1962). The Orsay center became a faculty in its own right in 1965 and the university Paris XI (Paris-Sud) in 1971.

The punchline. No one planned a science city south of Paris: each laboratory left the center for its own reasons (land, power, shielding, room to grow), and the Saclay plateau became the largest concentration of physics in France by accretion.

Electron-positron colliders: AdA and ACO

In this sectionThe small Italian ring that came to Orsay, and what it taught about colliding beams.

Bruno Touschek’s AdA (Anello di Accumulazione), one of the first electron-positron storage rings, was built at Frascati, near Rome, but its injector there was too weak. In 1962 it was brought to Orsay to be filled from the LAL linear accelerator, and in 1963 it produced the first observed electron-positron collisions. It also revealed the Touschek effect: particles in a bunch scatter off each other and are lost, which limits the lifetime of every stored beam since. The lab then built its own collider, ACO (1965), which studied the light vector mesons, followed by DCI (1976). Their synchrotron light, at first a nuisance, became a tool in itself: LURE (1973) used it for materials science, until its successor SOLEIL took over at Saint-Aubin. ACO is kept today as a museum on the Orsay campus.

From cosmic rays to astroparticles (1950–2026)

In this chapterHow Paris particle physicists left the mountains for accelerators in the 1950s, how space astrophysics grew out of rockets and the CEA, and how cosmic rays came back in the 2000s as astroparticle physics, with Paris laboratories in observatories on four continents and in space.

Leaving the mountains

In this sectionThe 1953 turn from cosmic rays to accelerators.

By the early 1950s most new particles had been found in cosmic rays, many by groups working at altitude. Leprince-Ringuet organized the 1953 international cosmic-ray conference at Bagnères-de-Bigorre, near the Pic du Midi. It is often remembered as the moment the field changed hands: the new accelerators, such as the Brookhaven Cosmotron, could make the same particles on demand, in large numbers and with a known energy. His laboratory turned to bubble chambers at accelerators, and two of its members, Bernard Gregory and André Lagarrigue, went on to lead CERN (Gregory as director-general, 1966–1970) and the Gargamelle collaboration.

Space astrophysics

In this sectionRockets, a space agency and the laboratories that build instruments for satellites.

The Service d’aéronomie of the CNRS (1958) studied the upper atmosphere with sounding rockets and became one of the first French space laboratories. The CNES followed in 1961, and the CEA started astrophysics at Saclay in partnership with it from the early 1960s. The Nançay radio station of the Observatoire (1953), in the Sologne, gave Paris radio astronomy. On the Orsay campus the Institut d’astrophysique spatiale (1990) built the HFI instrument of the Planck satellite, which mapped the cosmic microwave background, while at Meudon LESIA was one of the builders of the CoRoT satellite (2006), a pioneer in finding exoplanets by their transits. The IAP became a center of cosmology, and is one of the French hubs of the Euclid mission (2023).

The return of cosmic rays

In this sectionThe astroparticle laboratories, and a table of the observatories in which Paris groups take part.

From the 1990s, cosmic rays came back as astroparticle physics: the study of the highest-energy particles of the universe, gamma rays, neutrinos and gravitational waves, with the detectors and methods of particle physics. The Paris laboratories built for accelerators, LLR, LPNHE, LAL and IPN, took leading roles in the new observatories, and in 2005 a laboratory was created for the purpose: AstroParticule et Cosmologie (APC), which brought together the Collège de France group that descended from Joliot’s and Leprince-Ringuet’s laboratories with teams from the CEA, the Observatoire, the IAP and Orsay.

Break-up and reassembly (1968–2026)

In this chapterWhy the University of Paris was split in 1970, why laboratory names do not match university names, and how the pieces have been regrouped since 2018.

Thirteen universities and joint laboratories

In this sectionThe 1968 reform, the physics universities it produced, and the joint-laboratory system.

After May 1968 the Faure law replaced the faculties with autonomous, multidisciplinary universities. The University of Paris was dissolved and thirteen universities numbered Paris I to Paris XIII were created in 1970–71. Physics went mainly to Paris VI (later Pierre-et-Marie-Curie) and Paris VII (later Paris Diderot), which shared the new Jussieu campus; to Paris XI (Paris-Sud) at Orsay; and to Paris XIII at Villetaneuse.

The laboratories, meanwhile, became joint units belonging to a university and to the CNRS, and often also to the CEA, the Observatoire or a grande école. The IN2P3 (1971) gathered the CNRS nuclear and particle physics laboratories, wherever they were, into one national institute. This is why a laboratory such as LPNHE was at once a Paris VI and a CNRS laboratory, and why a map of laboratories never matched a map of universities.

Reassembly

In this sectionThe mergers of 2018–2020, and where the physics of the old university sits today.

In the 2000s the state encouraged regroupings, partly to give French universities the size that international rankings reward. Sorbonne Université (2018) merged Paris VI with the humanities of Paris IV. Paris VII, Paris V and the IPGP formed the Université de Paris (2020), renamed Université Paris Cité in 2022. Paris XI became the core of the Université Paris-Saclay (2020), with several grandes écoles and the CEA and CNRS laboratories of the plateau, and its five Orsay physics laboratories merged into IJCLab. The École normale, the Observatoire, ESPCI and others formed PSL, and Polytechnique leads the Institut Polytechnique de Paris (2019).
