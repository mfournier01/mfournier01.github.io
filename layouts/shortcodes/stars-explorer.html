Stellar particles in AthenaPK

The stars package turns dense gas into star particles, lets them explode as supernovae, and returns the ejecta, energy and momentum to the gas, all inside a block-structured, MPI-parallel, GPU-ready code. This page first explains the physics behind the model: how stars form and which gas can collapse, what a supernova remnant does and why a simulation must inject its terminal momentum when it cannot resolve the cooling shell. It then describes the computational choices that make the model conservative, deterministic and parallel-safe: a stochastic formation draw, a FIRE-2-style kernel with momentum-cancelling vector weights, a ghost swarm that carries feedback across meshblock and rank boundaries, a per-step density snapshot that removes order dependence, and counter-based random numbers.

It describes branch mfournier01/sfeed at commit 82806665; user-facing parameters are in docs/stars.md. Each particle is a simple stellar population with a fixed IMF. A ✦ marks a section with an interactive figure, and the rest of the page is built to be skimmed: click around.

Star formation

Stars form where gravity wins against everything that holds the gas up. A simulation at parsec resolution cannot follow the collapse itself, so the model keeps the physics that sets where and how fast it happens (support against gravity, a low efficiency per free-fall time) and replaces the rest by a stochastic draw (SMUGGLE; Marinacci et al. 2019).

How gas becomes stars

A patch of gas collapses when its self-gravity beats the support it has available. Four things matter:

Thermal pressure
Stops collapse below the Jeans length $\lambda_J=c_s\sqrt{\pi/G\rho}$. It is a strong barrier in warm gas ($c_s\sim10$ km/s) and a weak one in cold molecular gas ($c_s\sim0.2$ km/s).
Turbulence
Supersonic motions act as a non-thermal pressure. Their speed grows with scale, $\sigma\propto\ell^{1/2}$ (Larson 1981), so on cloud scales turbulence, not temperature, is what holds the gas up.
Magnetic fields
Add pressure and tension on top of the two above. The Alfvén variant of the virial test includes them through $v_A^2=B^2/\rho$.
Feedback
Winds, radiation and supernovae from the first stars stir and disperse the gas again, which keeps the global efficiency low.

The balance is measured by the virial parameter $\alpha_{\rm vir}\sim2E_{\rm kin}/|E_{\rm grav}|$ (Bertoldi and McKee 1992): gas with $\alpha_{\rm vir}\lesssim1$ is bound and collapses. Observed molecular clouds sit near $\alpha_{\rm vir}\sim1$ to 2, held up by turbulence, yet they turn only about 1% of their mass into stars per free-fall time (Krumholz and Tan 2007), much slower than free collapse. Turbulence compresses gas into dense bound clumps that collapse, while the rest is stirred and rebounds.

The code sees none of this below the cell size. It keeps three subgrid ingredients:

  1. Is the gas bound? A test on the resolved cell compares gravity with thermal and turbulent support, using the velocity gradients of the simulation (Sect. Virial test).
  2. How fast does it form stars? A Schmidt law, $\dot\rho_\star=\varepsilon\rho/t_{\rm ff}$, with $\varepsilon$ the efficiency per free-fall time, absorbing everything unresolved (Sect. Rate and the random draw).
  3. Which cell, and when? A random draw turns gas into a star particle, so that the average rate matches the law (same section).
How fast the gas is used up interactive

$t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{3\pi/32G\rho}$ falls as $\rho^{-1/2}$, so the rate per volume, $\varepsilon\rho/t_{\rm ff}\propto\rho^{3/2}$, is steep: dense gas forms stars much faster than diffuse gas. With $\varepsilon\approx1\%$, even gas at $10^2\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ takes a few hundred Myr to be used up.

Which gas can form stars: the virial test

With sf_virial_criterion_enabled = true a candidate cell ($\rho>\rho_{\rm th}$, sf_density_threshold) must also be gravitationally bound (CheckVirialCollapse); a cell that fails has $P=0$ in the draw below. The default is the cell-sized virial parameter of Hopkins et al. (2013), used by FIRE-2 and SMUGGLE:

\alpha=\frac{\overbrace{\|\nabla\otimes\mathbf v\|^2}^{\text{turbulence and shear}}+\overbrace{(c_s/\Delta x)^2}^{\text{thermal}}}{8\pi G\rho}\ \le\ \alpha_{\rm crit}

The first term is the support from the resolved velocity field, taken from centred differences of the primitive variables: turbulent motions, shear and rotation across the cell. The second is thermal support, with $\Delta x$ as the length scale. Together they are compared with gravity. Whatever turbulence the grid cannot resolve is not counted, which is the reason for the efficiency $\varepsilon$ in the rate.

What holds the gas up at the scale of a cell? interactive

Turbulence follows a Larson-like cascade, $\sigma(\ell)=\sigma_1(\ell/{\rm pc})^{1/2}$, standing in for the velocity field a real run would resolve; mean molecular weight 0.6. Below the sonic scale $\ell_s=c_s^2/\sigma_1^2$ thermal support dominates, above it turbulence does. Gas is bound where the total is under $\alpha_{\rm crit}=1$.

Three details matter. The velocity-gradient norm sums all nine components (six in 2D), and converging flows count as support, as in the original definition. The thermal term alone sets an effective density threshold, $\rho>c_s^2/(8\pi G\Delta x^2)$, about $6\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$ at $10^4$ K and $\Delta x=100$ pc, and rising as $\Delta x^{-2}$ at higher resolution (the readout above shows it). And the Cen and Ostriker test uses a temperature cut in place of the paper's $t_{\rm cool}<t_{\rm dyn}$, so $T_{\rm th}$ must sit above the cooling-table floor or it will rarely pass. The history SFR and both injection passes use exactly the same tests, so they always select the same cells.

Rate and the random draw

A bound, dense cell forms stars at the Schmidt rate, with $\varepsilon=$ sf_efficiency the fraction of its gas converted per free-fall time:

\dot M_\star=\varepsilon\,\frac{M_{\rm gas}}{t_{\rm ff}},\qquad t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho}},\qquad M_{\rm gas}=\rho\,\Delta V

The cell cannot hand out this mass continuously: a star particle is a lump. So each step the cell forms one star, of mass $\varepsilon_m M_{\rm gas}$ (sf_mass_efficiency), with probability (Springel and Hernquist 2003)

P=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{1-\exp\!\left(-\dot M_\star\Delta t/M_{\rm gas}\right)}{\varepsilon_m}\right)

The numerator is the gas fraction a continuous law would convert over $\Delta t$. Dividing by $\varepsilon_m$ makes the expected mass formed equal to it, so the average rate follows the Schmidt law whatever the particle mass: a smaller $\varepsilon_m$ gives more and lighter stars, not a different rate. This holds as long as $P<1$, i.e. $\varepsilon_m\gtrsim\varepsilon\Delta t/t_{\rm ff}$. The star_formation_rate history output uses the continuous rate, which exceeds the per-step expectation by about $\varepsilon\Delta t/2t_{\rm ff}$ (about 2% in the SFR test).

What happens to the gas and the star

TransferCellMassToParticle moves $\varepsilon_m\rho\Delta V$ into the new star. The star sits at the cell centre, takes the cell's velocity (all three components even in 2D), and records its birth time, birth mass and birth position (birth_x/y/z, used later for the random draws). The gas density, all three momenta and the passive-scalar densities are multiplied by $1-\varepsilon_m$, so velocities and scalar concentrations are unchanged; magnetic fields are never touched.

Two passes per block

InjectParticles (shared with the tracer particles) cannot know in advance how many stars a block will make, and the swarm must be sized before it is filled. So it runs the same decision twice: a first pass counts the cells that form a star, the swarm is then allocated, and a second pass fills it. This only works because the random draw is a deterministic function of its inputs (Sect. Randomness and IDs): both passes reach exactly the same decisions. An atomic counter gives each new star its slot and its ID offset.

Supernova feedback

A supernova releases about $10^{51}$ erg into a few to tens of solar masses of ejecta, but almost all of that energy is radiated away within a fraction of a million years. What a galaxy actually feels is the momentum the blast wave leaves behind. The model draws each star's explosions from the IMF and the delay-time distribution, and couples them to the gas through a kernel that reproduces this momentum even when the cooling shell cannot be resolved (FIRE-2 and SMUGGLE; Hopkins et al. 2018).

Who explodes, and when

Two channels matter. Core-collapse supernovae (Type II) are the deaths of stars above about $8M_\odot$, so they follow star formation within 3 to 40 Myr. Type Ia supernovae are thermonuclear explosions of white dwarfs in binaries; they need the white dwarf to exist first, and then trail off slowly over Gyr. Each step, every star draws how many of each happen in $[{\rm age},{\rm age}+\Delta t]$, as a Poisson number keyed on the star's birth properties (Sect. Randomness and IDs).

Each event returns the IMF-mean ejecta of the window, using the Portinari remnant masses.

Anatomy of a supernova remnant

The ejecta leave at about 3000 km/s with a few to tens of $M_\odot$, and then the remnant goes through four stages (McKee and Ostriker 1977; Cioffi et al. 1988). Their order is set by one question: how much gas has the blast swept up, and has it had time to cool?

A supernova remnant, from the explosion to the merger play it, or click a phase
The radius of the sketch is drawn on a compressed scale; arrows show the momentum.

The key point is the bottom bar. In the energy-conserving stage the hot bubble keeps pushing the shell, doing $p\,dV$ work: the momentum grows as the shell collects mass, while the thermal energy is stored in the bubble. Once the shell cools, that stored energy is radiated away within a few $t_{\rm sf}$, but the momentum the shell already has is conserved. The final momentum, not the energy, is what stirs the interstellar medium and drives outflows.

Resolved or not: the two momenta

A simulation with cells of a few parsecs or more cannot see the phases above. The ejecta of a single SN share a few cells with a few hundred $M_\odot$ of gas, the shell is thinner than a cell, and a hot bubble placed there would radiate its energy numerically in a few steps (overcooling). So the code gives the gas the momentum the remnant would have reached, and it needs two reference values:

\begin{aligned}p_{\rm SN}&=\sqrt{2\,E\,M_{\rm ej}}\ \approx\ 3\times10^{4}\,M_\odot\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\,\left(\frac{M_{\rm ej}}{10M_\odot}\right)^{1/2}&&\text{what the ejecta carry,}\\ p_t&\simeq4.8\times10^5\,M_\odot\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\ N_{\rm SN}^{13/14}\,\langle n_{\rm H}\rangle^{-1/7}&&\text{what the remnant ends with}\end{aligned}
$p_{\rm SN}$: the start
The momentum of the ejecta themselves in free expansion, fixed by the star: its energy $E=N_{\rm SN}E_{\rm SN}$ and its ejecta mass. It is what the code can inject exactly at the first step.
$p_t$: the end
The terminal momentum after the shell has formed and the energy is gone (Cioffi et al. 1988). It depends on the energy $E\propto N_{\rm SN}$ and weakly on the density, $\langle n_{\rm H}\rangle^{-1/7}$, and is about 15 times larger than $p_{\rm SN}$ for a single SN in gas at $1\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$.
In between: the boost
While the blast is energy-conserving, swept-up mass $m_{\rm sw}$ multiplies the momentum by $\sqrt{1+m_{\rm sw}/M_{\rm ej}}$ (kinetic energy fixed at $E$ while the mass grows). The code evaluates it with each cell's own mass $m_b$.
What the grid can follow, and what the code injects interactive

Solid: the physical remnant (as above, $M_{\rm ej}=10M_\odot$ per SN). Dashed: the code, $\min\big(p_{\rm SN}\sqrt{1+m_{\rm sw}/M_{\rm ej}},\ p_t\big)$ with $m_{\rm sw}$ the gas mass inside the radius. The boost assumes all the energy stays kinetic, so it runs a little above the true Sedov value (about 28% of $E$ is in motion); the cap at $p_t$ keeps the total right.

The rule is simple: inject the momentum of the energy-conserving stage, and stop at $p_t$. If the kernel is smaller than the shell-formation radius $R_{\rm sf}$ the boost is below the cap, the injection is almost purely kinetic, and the hydro solver continues the evolution itself, with its cooling. If the kernel is larger than $R_{\rm sf}$, the cooling would act inside a few cells and cannot be resolved, so the momentum is capped at $p_t$, and the leftover energy is added as thermal energy, which the gas is free to radiate.

One event per star per step

The events of one star in one step are merged into one event with $N_{\rm SN}=N_{\rm II}+N_{\rm Ia}$, energy $E=N_{\rm SN}E_{\rm SN}$ (E_SN_per_event), the ejecta mass $M_{\rm ej}$, and the two momenta above, with $p_{\rm SN}=\sum_{\rm type}\sqrt{2N\,E_1\,M_{\rm ej,type}}$ summed over the Type II and Type Ia contributions. In $p_t$ the density $\langle n_{\rm H}\rangle$ is the kernel-weighted hydrogen density around the star, read from the density snapshot. If $M_{\rm ej}$ exceeds the star's remaining mass, the ejecta (and $p_{\rm SN}$ with them) are scaled down to what is left and the star is removed after the step.

The kernel and its vector weights

Each event is spread over a sphere around the star, with smoothing length $h=\tfrac12(r+1)\Delta x_{\rm host}$ and $r=$ SN_injection_radius_cells. The support is $2h$, so with the default $r=2$ the kernel is $3\Delta x$ in radius. Because $h$ is set by the host cell's resolution, the physical footprint does not change if a neighbour is refined. Cell $b$ gets the scalar weight $w_b=W(r_b,h)\Delta V_b$ with $W$ the M4 cubic spline.

Momentum needs direction. The FIRE-2 vector weights (Hopkins et al. 2018, eqs. 9 and 10) point each cell's share away from the star, $\bar{\mathbf w}_{b,a}=w_b\hat x_{b,a}f^\pm_a$, with the factors $f^\pm_a$ computed from sums over the whole kernel so that $\sum_b\bar{\mathbf w}_b=0$ exactly, even when the star is off its cell centre: the injected momenta cancel and no spurious net momentum is created. The scalar share $s_b=|\bar{\mathbf w}_b|/\sum|\bar{\mathbf w}|$ sets the cell's mass and energy and normalizes its momentum. A cell with the star exactly at its centre has no direction: it gets mass and energy but no momentum.

Coupling to a cell

ApplyKineticSNe gives cell $b$, in the star's frame, a fixed mass share and energy share and a momentum with the boost and the cap of the previous section:

\begin{aligned}\Delta m_b&=s_bM_{\rm ej},\qquad \Delta E_b=s_bE\quad(\text{never changed by the momentum}),\\ \Delta\mathbf p_b&=\frac{\bar{\mathbf w}_b}{\sum|\bar{\mathbf w}|}\,\min\!\left(p_{\rm SN}\sqrt{1+m_b/\Delta m_b},\ p_t\right)\end{aligned}

Here $m_b$ is read from the density snapshot. Everything is then moved to the simulation frame (Hopkins et al. 2018, eqs. 23 and 24), with $\mathbf v_\star$ the star's velocity:

\begin{aligned}\Delta(\rho\mathbf v)&=\Delta\mathbf p_b+\Delta m_b\mathbf v_\star,\\ \Delta E_{\rm tot}&=\Delta E_b+\Delta\mathbf p_b\cdot\mathbf v_\star+\tfrac12\Delta m_bv_\star^2\end{aligned}

The total energy is exactly $E_{\rm SN}$ in the star's frame plus the kinetic energy carried by the ejecta, in both regimes. What differs is how it splits: in the uncapped regime the kinetic energy $p^2/2(m+\Delta m)$ equals the cell's share of $E$, so the injection is almost purely kinetic; in the capped regime the kinetic energy falls below the share and the remainder goes in as thermal energy, where the cooling can act on it.

Because the boost differs from cell to cell, a small net momentum is left: about 0.02% at 2 pc and 0.5% at 4 pc ($n_{\rm H}=1$). After all deposits of the step, ApplyInternalEnergyFloor raises any cell whose thermal energy went below the hydro floor (pfloor, Tfloor, or the smallest positive number). That can happen when gas moving towards the star is pushed back, or when the star moves fast relative to the gas: the correction factors of Hopkins et al.'s Appendix E are not implemented.

Three passes in ApplyStellarFeedback

All feedback quantities of an event are computed once, then used by both the local deposit and the ghost particles, so the two cannot disagree. Exhausted stars are removed only after the third pass, so their ghost particles still see them.

Crossing block boundaries, and staying reproducible

A feedback kernel reaches into neighbouring meshblocks, which may live on other MPI ranks. The solution is a second swarm of ghost stars, together with a frozen density snapshot and counter-based random numbers, so that results do not depend on the number of ranks, the order of threads or restarts.

The ghost swarm

The problem. A block can only write its own interior cells, and a kernel near a block face, edge or corner needs cells owned by up to seven other blocks. The approach. Instead of a custom message, the module reuses Parthenon's swarm communication, in four steps:

  1. Find the neighbours. DetectKernelOverlap walks the kernel cells and records, along each axis, whether any weighted cell lies beyond the block's interior: $n=0$ to $3$ active axes give $2^n-1$ neighbour regions. The very same walk serves the counting and the filling passes, so they always agree (a mismatch aborts with "slot counter mismatch").
  2. Split the budgets. ComputeRegionFractions sums the scalar weights of the kernel cells for each region, at the host's resolution, using the host block's face coordinates. This gives fractions $f_m$ with $\sum_mf_m=1$.
  3. Send. Each ghost particle sits at the star's position pushed across the shared face(s) by r_search cells, with the push vector stored (offset_x/y/z). Parthenon's ownership check sends it to the right neighbour, locally or over MPI, in the same send/receive round as the moving stars. It carries $f_m$ times the event's budgets, $h$, $f^\pm$ and the star's velocity.
  4. Receive (ApplyGhostFeedback). The block subtracts the offset to recover the true star position, outside its interior, runs ApplyKineticSNe over its own cells with the event's $h$ and $f^\pm$, and normalizes over the cells it owns. Then it removes the ghost and applies the energy floor.

Properties.

Conservation
Each region deposits exactly $f_m$ of the event's mass and energy, so totals are conserved. Tested: a star at a block corner is identical to one in the interior, and 1, 2 and 8 MPI ranks give identical results.
Same-level neighbours
Every region normalizes over exactly the cells the host counted for it, so the vector weights still cancel and the result equals a single-block deposit.
Boundaries
Periodic: Parthenon wraps a pushed ghost's position, and subtracting the offset gives the periodic image of the star, the right centre for the receiving block. Outflow: a ghost pushed out of the domain is dropped, so the share outside the box is lost, like gas flowing out.

The density snapshot

At the start of ApplyStellarFeedback each block copies its density, ghost cells included, into a separate field sn_rho_snapshot: the density after star formation and before any SN deposit of this step. Every density read of the feedback uses it, including the ghost deposits received later: the swept mass $m_b$ of the boost and the $\langle n_{\rm H}\rangle$ that scales $p_t$.

Why. Several events can touch the same cells in one step (neighbouring stars, or a host deposit and an incoming ghost). If each read the live density, it would see the mass the previous one just deposited, and the result would depend on the order of the events, which is nondeterministic on GPUs and under MPI. With the snapshot every event sees the same state, the outcome is order-independent, and the atomic additions still sum exactly. The approximation is that overlapping kernels in the same step ignore each other's ejecta in the boost, negligible unless SNe are strongly clustered in space and time. The cost is one scalar field, written once per step, allocated only when SN feedback is on and not stored in restart files.

Limits at refinement jumps

Neighbour at a different level
The fractions $f_m$ are computed on the host's grid while the neighbour deposits on its own. Mass and energy are still conserved exactly, but the momenta no longer cancel: a net momentum toward the coarse side, up to 1.6% of the radial momentum, was measured with the star on the fine side.
Coarse host, several fine neighbours
(From reading the code; untested.) The region across a coarse face can span up to four fine blocks, but the host only knows its own faces. If the kernel also straddles an internal boundary between them, the share goes to the one fine block that receives the ghost. Nothing is lost, but the deposit is concentrated on one side; it needs the star within about $3\Delta x$ of the fine blocks' shared edge.
Kernel larger than a block
The kernel must not reach past the immediate neighbours: the meshblock must be at least about r_search cells wide, easily true for blocks of 8 cells or more.
Positivity
The near-pure-kinetic injection can produce negative thermal energy when cells are refined or their ghost cells are filled from a coarser level. Runs with refinement need hydro/Tfloor, and adaptive refinement can then add a few percent of the SN energy through the floor.

Randomness and IDs

All random numbers come from counter-based hashes (custom_rng.hpp), not from random-number streams. Each draw is a pure function of its inputs, so results do not depend on thread order, MPI layout or restarts.

SN draws are keyed on birth properties, not on the particle ID, for two reasons: under adaptive refinement IDs cannot be kept unique (every star then gets the dummy ID UINT64_MAX), and a restarted run must reproduce the same SN sequence. Star formation rate

The first test has no supernovae. It fills a box with 16,384 identical overdense cells ($\varepsilon=0.5$, $\varepsilon_m=0.75$, $t_{\rm ff}=14.8$ Myr) and lets them form stars through the stochastic draw. The gas of each cell is used up at a known rate, so the star formation rate and the total stellar mass follow an exponential law that the star particles must reproduce:

\dot M_\star(t)=N_{\rm pk}\,\varepsilon_m m_{\rm pk}\,\lambda\,e^{-\lambda t},\qquad \lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_m t_{\rm ff}}

Three things are compared: the analytic law above, the exact per-step expectation computed from the gas state at the start of each step ($P\,\varepsilon_mM_{\rm gas}=M_{\rm gas}(1-e^{-x})$ with $x=\varepsilon\Delta t/t_{\rm ff}$), and the stellar mass actually measured from the birth mass and injection time of the star particles.

A supernova at a block corner

The core test of the feedback coupling is a single forced supernova (SN_test_event_age, $10^{51}$ erg, $16.8M_\odot$ of ejecta) in uniform gas ($n_{\rm H}=1\ {\rm cm^{-3}}$, $T=100$ K, adiabatic, $\Delta x=2$ pc). The star sits at the corner shared by eight meshblocks, and off the centre of its host cell, so the kernel crosses every face and the ghost swarm is exercised at full strength. The reference is the Sedov–Taylor similarity solution, which for $\gamma=5/3$ has

R_{\rm ST}=\xi_0\left(\frac{E\,t^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5},\qquad p_{\rm ST}=c_p\,E^{4/5}\rho_0^{1/5}\,t^{3/5}

with $\xi_0=1.152$ and a kinetic energy fraction of 0.28, integrated numerically from the self-similar equations. The same run in a single meshblock, with the star at the same position, shows what the block boundaries change. The shock radius is measured in each octant where the angle-binned pressure falls to half its peak; the band is the spread between octants.

The two runs are indistinguishable, and both end within 2% of the analytic radius and momentum at 300 kyr. At early times the blast is not resolved, so the radius overshoots and the momentum sits above the similarity solution: this is the energy-conserving boost of the momentum injection at work. The kinetic and thermal energies settle at the Sedov–Taylor fractions. The total energy of the eight-block run is 1% lower than injected, a hydro effect of the first-order flux correction across meshblock faces, not of the stars: the single-block run conserves it to $10^{-6}$.

Resolution

The same blast is run in $64^3$ cells with boxes from 64 to 1024 pc, so $\Delta x$ goes from 1 to 16 pc and the star keeps the same offset in its cell. The question is how fast the answer approaches Sedov–Taylor as the resolution improves, and where the terminal-momentum cap takes over.

Top right: relative difference at 50 kyr from the 1 pc run, with the fitted slope between 2 and 8 pc. Bottom: the share of the injected energy that is kinetic at the first step, and the energy at the end of the run.

Two features stand out. Early on, each run lies above the similarity solution, and the excess lasts until the radius is about ten cells: the injection hands over the momentum of an energy-conserving blast (all energy kinetic), but the true remnant keeps only 28% of it in motion, and the hydro solver needs a few cells to relax. And at 8 and especially 16 pc the injected energy is no longer fully kinetic (94% and 58%): the cell masses are so large that the boost would exceed $p_t$, the momentum is capped, and the rest goes in as thermal energy. All resolutions converge to the same answer after the kernel has been left behind.

Cooling against adiabatic

The cooling test repeats the blast at 2 pc with and without radiative cooling (Schure collisional ionization equilibrium table, solar metallicity), in a larger box. The adiabatic run must stay on Sedov–Taylor forever; the cooling run must leave it when the shell forms, at about $t_{\rm sf}\approx44$ kyr and $R_{\rm sf}\approx22$ pc (Kim and Ostriker 2015, KO15), and the momentum must plateau at their $p_{\rm sf}=2.17\times10^5M_\odot$ km/s.

Radiative cooling switches the blast from energy-conserving to momentum-conserving interactive

The adiabatic run follows the similarity solution, with the kinetic and thermal energies at their Sedov–Taylor fractions. In the cooling run the thermal energy collapses between 20 and 40 kyr, the shell forms where KO15 predict, and the momentum flattens at about $2.1\times10^5M_\odot$ km/s, which is the value of $p_{\rm sf}$. It then rises again towards $3\times10^5$, which comes from the setup: the cooling table stops at $1.6\times10^4$ K while the ambient gas is at 100 K. Note that the code's $p_t$ for this event ($4.8\times10^5$) is about 2.2 times the plateau.

Refinement

The last blast tests ask whether the kernel and ghost swarm behave at a change of resolution. The same SN is run on a uniform 2 pc grid, a uniform 4 pc grid, a static mesh with a 4 pc coarse and a 2 pc fine region (the star in the coarse or in the fine host, so the kernel crosses the level jump either way), and an adaptively refined run that follows the blast.

The blast across resolution jumps interactive

Density in the plane of the star at an early time (top, zoomed) and at 100 kyr (bottom). The star is the white marker; the dotted circle is the Sedov–Taylor radius for the same energy and density. The thin lines are the meshblock edges.

Static refinement
Energy is deposited exactly, and the shock stays symmetric to within the difference in resolution. The injection leaves a net momentum toward the coarse side, up to 1.6% of $p_r$; this is one of the known limits (Sect. Limits at refinement jumps).
Adaptive refinement
The star survives refinement of its own region. Because the injection is nearly pure kinetic energy, refined runs need hydro/Tfloor; the floor adds about 6% of $E_{\rm SN}$ when the blast refines.
A hydro caveat
The first-order flux correction of the hydro solver loses about 1% of the energy across meshblock faces. It affects all blast runs with several blocks and is not a property of the stars package.

Ranks and restart

Two last tests check that the result does not depend on how the run is executed. The corner blast was run in serial and on 1, 2 and 8 MPI ranks, and the adaptive run was restarted from a checkpoint in the middle of the blast. Their history files were compared row by row.
